## Overview

Some people are walking trivia machines. Ask them anything and the answer arrives, cross-referenced and contextualized. Others read the same articles, sit through the same lectures, and forget most of it within a week. What's the difference?

This article distills a short video by Magpie of *Reading is a Skill*, who has been told his whole life that he has a memory "like a steel trap." After studying cognitive science, he attributes it to three factors — but argues that one of them, *thinking in frameworks*, is the most powerful and the most replicable. The thesis: facts don't stick on their own. They stick when they fit into — or pointedly contradict — a framework you already hold.

If that's true, then the question of how to remember more facts collapses into a different question: how do you hold more frameworks?

![Magpie holding Michael Polanyi's "The Logic of Liberty"](/blog/frameworks-determine-facts/polanyi-logic-of-liberty-book.jpg)

## Three Factors Behind a "Memory Like a Steel Trap"

Magpie credits three things for his unusual fact retention:

**1. Probable synesthesia.** He picked up the idea from Nassim Nicholas Taleb, who mentioned having synesthesia in *Antifragile*. Synesthesia means associating sensory information in ways most people don't — and a common variant is vivid spatial visualization. Magpie describes thinking the way characters on TV are sometimes depicted: arranging concepts in mental space, even if he isn't gesturing in front of him. He notes this is close to the *memory palace* technique that competitive memorizers train themselves to use.

**2. Emotional salience, especially negative.** People remember emotionally charged events more durably, and negative emotion tends to be especially sticky. Magpie has episodic memories from before age three — unusual, since most people's earliest memories are around seven. He attributes this to emotional intensity. He also scored at the 99th percentile for neuroticism on the Big Five personality inventory for most of his life (though that has dropped since his father died).

**3. Thinking in frameworks.** This is the factor he wants to focus on, because it's the one most people can deliberately cultivate.

## What a Framework Is — and Isn't

As an undergraduate, Magpie called all of these things *heuristics*. He has since refined the vocabulary:

- A **heuristic** is a rule of thumb. Often it's a smaller piece of a larger framework.
- A **framework** is "a way of looking at the world" — a map of a particular piece of territory.
- A **paradigm**, in Thomas Kuhn's sense from *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, overlaps heavily with what he means by framework.

The critical move is recognizing that *the territory and the map are different things, and more than one map can describe the same territory.* Most people, he observes, hold exactly one map per piece of territory. They've been taught it, it works, and they assume it's the territory itself.

## Frameworks You Reject Are Still Worth Understanding

Magpie illustrates the multiple-maps idea with a personal example. As a young person, encountering Young Earth Creationism is a big part of why he stopped believing in God for about 20 years. The televangelists defending it weren't just citing the Bible incidentally — they were operating from a coherent system built on three doctrines:

- *Sola scriptura* (scripture as the sole authority)
- *Biblical literalism*
- *Biblical inerrancy*

The adults around him kept reassuring him that Young Earth Creationism didn't contradict the Bible — but they were missing his actual question, which was about the *mechanisms* the televangelists were using. So he got interested in theology even though he didn't believe.

What he found: if you genuinely commit to all three of those doctrines, your reading of the Bible would land close to the **Christadelphians**. The vast majority of Protestants who profess sola scriptura, literalism, and inerrancy don't actually hold the distinctively Christadelphian conclusions — which is itself revealing. And Young Earth Creationism, on its own terms, *hangs together as a system*, including the supposedly scientific parts. He thought it was wrong, but he could see the coherence.

The point isn't that any framework is as good as any other. The point is that *coherence is portable*: you can model how a system works internally without endorsing it.

## Polanyi: Science Is One Framework Among Many

Magpie has recently been reading **Michael Polanyi** — specifically *The Logic of Liberty*, which he picked up from Liberty Fund for around $15 as a print-on-demand edition. Polanyi has been near the top of his reading list for a long time but was usually expensive; he eventually bought a couple of titles used.

Polanyi's argument, as Magpie summarizes it: if you want to understand science, you have to understand that science is *one framework* for looking at the world. We choose to believe it's the best framework for answering empirical questions. But witchcraft, astrology, and other rejected frameworks are still *internally coherent* to the people who hold them. Science might one day be replaced, or rejected frameworks might return — and we should keep that possibility in mind.

This isn't relativism. It's an argument for epistemic humility about *which* framework we're inside.

## How Frameworks Govern What You Remember

Here's the mechanism, as Magpie describes it. The facts that stick are the ones that have a relationship to a framework you hold. Specifically:

- **Coherent facts stick** when they're the obvious conclusion of the framework. (Though he admits he may not really be remembering these — he may be inferring them in the moment, the way an LLM would, because the framework predicts them.)
- **Counterintuitive facts stick** when they contradict what the framework predicts. The contradiction makes them stand out.
- **Bland facts get forgotten** when they're consistent with the predictions of *several* frameworks at once. Nothing about them is distinctive, so nothing flags them for retention.

If this is right, the failure mode of pure fact-accumulation isn't laziness — it's structural. Without a framework, even the most diligent learner has no scaffold to hang anything on.

## Without a Framework, You Don't Even Hear the Argument

Magpie ties this to a striking observation from Daniel Everett's *Don't Sleep, There Are Snakes*: the Pirahã people report seeing supernatural creatures that outsiders simply can't see. Polanyi notes a related phenomenon — we don't see the blind spot in our visual field, we don't notice the floaters in our eyes, we're used to *not seeing what we expect not to see*.

The cognitive version: without a framework that can accommodate new information, you tend not to process it. Magpie notes that when people would present him with arguments outside any framework he held, he wouldn't even understand the argument. He would unconsciously substitute the argument he *thought* they were making and respond to that instead.

This is the perceptual cost of holding only one map.

## Most Disputes Aren't About Facts

A consequence: most disagreements aren't really fights about *what the facts are*. They're fights about *how to prioritize and interpret facts everyone roughly agrees on*.

There may be some genuine factual dispute at the edges. There's plenty of pedantry. But the bulk of the argument is usually about how to weave the same set of facts into different interpretations — and *no set of facts implies an interpretation on its own*. You need a framework to do that work, and people tend to assume that the framework they were handed in school or by their community is the one they should use.

Recognize that there are multiple frameworks, and the argument becomes legible in a different way.

## Education Should Teach Facts Inside Frameworks

Some commentators — Magpie cites **J.J. McCullough** as an example — argue that the most important thing in education is accumulating facts. Magpie thinks fact accumulation matters, especially early on, but it has to happen *inside the frameworks that make the facts useful*. Otherwise, you're loading up a warehouse with no shelving.

The frameworks aren't an academic flourish. They're what turn isolated trivia into something a person can actually deploy.

## Frameworks Let You Self-Correct

The payoff at the end of the video is subtle and worth lingering on. If you only hold one framework per area of life, you can't really self-correct — you have nothing to compare it against. But if you hold several, including ones you find morally offensive or epistemologically indefensible, you can still draw insight from them. A framework you reject can shed light on the one you've adopted, expose blind spots, and let you say: *the framework I was following isn't quite right.*

You can modify it. You can absorb the partial truth out of a system you'd never endorse wholesale. That's the kind of self-correction that's basically unavailable to someone with one map.

## Key Takeaways

- **Memory follows structure.** Facts stick when they have a relationship to a framework — either fitting it or breaking it. Loose facts evaporate.
- **A framework is a map, not the territory.** Most people hold one map per piece of territory. Holding more is a learnable skill.
- **Coherence is portable.** You can model frameworks you reject — Young Earth Creationism, astrology, witchcraft — without endorsing them. Internal coherence is information.
- **Disputes are usually about prioritization.** Two parties often agree on the facts and disagree about which framework should weight them.
- **No framework, no perception.** Without a framework that can hold new information, you tend not to even process it.
- **Multiple frameworks enable self-correction.** A framework you reject can still expose flaws in the one you hold.
