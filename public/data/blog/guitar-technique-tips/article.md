## Overview

What if you could learn everything you need to master classical guitar technique from just one exercise? According to experienced classical guitarist Ariel, all guitar playing reduces to two fundamental finger motions: an inward plucking gesture and its opposite outward motion. Whether you use two, three, or four fingers, these are the only movements you need to calibrate.

This approach challenges conventional wisdom about slow practice. The key insight: practicing slowly first actually teaches incorrect technique 99% of the time. Instead, start fast (even if messy), slow down briefly to refine, then speed up again to test. This counterintuitive method ensures you're learning motions that actually work at performance speed.

## The Two Core Motions

All classical guitar technique boils down to two gestures:

1. **Inward pluck** - fingers curl toward the palm
2. **Outward release** - the opposite motion

Every technique—arpeggios, tremolo, scales, rest strokes—derives from these same two movements. The goal is to calibrate these motions through deliberate repetition until they produce clear, consistent sound.

## Nail Preparation is Essential

Before any practice, your nails must be properly prepared:

- **Length**: Keep nails relatively short; too long causes problems
- **Shape**: File the inner OR outer edge of the nail (not both)
- **Polish**: Use 1000+ grit sandpaper to smooth the surface

The metallic, harsh sound many beginners produce comes from the nail contacting the string at two points simultaneously. Playing with either the inner or outer edge alone produces a cleaner tone.

## The Right Hand Stays Relentless

This is the critical principle: **your right hand never stops**. While practicing, the right hand maintains its rhythm regardless of mistakes or synchronization issues. The left hand "catches up" and synchronizes to the right.

Why this matters:
- Stopping to correct mistakes teaches you to stop
- The relentless right hand forces adaptation
- Synchronization happens through repetition, not correction

## Practice Fast First, Then Clean Up

The conventional wisdom of "start slow, build speed gradually" is backwards for technique development:

- **Slow practice teaches wrong technique** - The motions you use at slow speeds don't work at fast speeds
- **Fast practice reveals correct technique** - If it doesn't work fast, the technique is wrong
- **The process**: Go fast (messy is okay) → slow down briefly → speed up to test → repeat

The goal isn't mindless repetition. Each attempt should be slightly different as you search for the motion that works. If you're doing the exact same thing each time, you're not learning.

## Pressure Over Motion

When playing on inner strings (2nd, 3rd), space is limited. You can't make large finger movements. The solution: focus on pressure rather than motion.

Even with minimal finger travel—starting from the string itself—you can generate full volume by applying pressure. This is how you maintain sound quality when space constraints limit your movement.

## Include the Pinky

Even if you never use the pinky in performance, practice with it anyway. Training the pinky fine-tunes overall hand coordination, making your other fingers more precise. The gesture is the same; it's not harder, just unfamiliar.

## Constant Tone Fingering

For consistent tone across a passage, match your right-hand position to your left-hand position. The principle: strike each note at the same proportional position on the string.

As you move up the fretboard (shortening the string), your right hand should shift toward the bridge. This keeps the tone consistent because you're always playing at the same fraction of the string length—not the same physical location.

## Breaking Down the Exercise

Practice these combinations to calibrate all finger interactions:

- **Full sequence**: Pinky, ring, middle, index, thumb (then reverse)
- **Three-finger patterns**: Ring-middle-index, thumb-index-middle
- **Two-finger patterns**: Index-middle, index-ring, ring-middle
- **Arpeggio patterns**: Circular arpeggios on single strings

Start on the first string where space is abundant, then move to inner strings where the challenge increases. The difficulty of finding space and maintaining synchronization is part of the learning process.

## Key Takeaways

- All classical guitar technique reduces to two finger motions
- Fast practice teaches correct technique; slow-only practice teaches wrong technique
- The right hand stays relentless; the left hand synchronizes to it
- Proper nail preparation is non-negotiable
- Use pressure, not motion, when space is limited
- Practice with the pinky even if you won't perform with it
- Match right-hand position to left-hand position for consistent tone
- Deliberate repetition while watching entertainment makes practice sustainable
