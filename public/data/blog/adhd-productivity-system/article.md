## Why Most Productivity Advice Fails

Most productivity content gets it backwards. Add this app. Use this template. Buy this course on building elaborate systems.

But productivity isn't about adding more—it's about removing what's in the way.

Ali Alqaraghuli, a systems engineer at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, built a framework that reflects this reality. He completed his PhD at 26, works at NASA, runs two companies, and still maintains a life outside work. His system isn't complicated. It's focused on what actually matters.

---

## Step Zero: Subtract Before You Add

Before implementing any productivity system, you need to identify and remove the things draining your time and attention.

Think of it this way: if you're trying to grab a mug but something's blocking your hand, you have to move the obstacle first. No amount of technique for "better mug grabbing" will help until the path is clear.

Common obstacles to audit:
- Phone and social media
- Unnecessary commitments
- Toxic or draining relationships
- Digital clutter (tabs, apps, notifications)

Spend an hour listing what's consuming your attention without giving returns. Then start removing.

Only after subtraction does addition make sense.

## The Four Pillars of the ADHD System

The "Add" step is a productivity system built on four pillars, particularly useful for those higher on the ADHD spectrum—people whose brains generate too many ideas, struggle with focus, and get bored easily.

### Pillar 1: Clarity

You need a clear goal and a clear reason for pursuing it.

Without clarity, you circle endlessly. You feel busy but make no progress. Clarity isn't just knowing what you want—it's knowing why you want it.

Ask yourself:
- What specific outcome am I working toward?
- Why does this matter to me?
- How will I know when I've achieved it?

### Pillar 2: Prioritization

Once you have clarity, identify what's actually blocking your progress.

Prioritization isn't about ranking your to-do list. It's about finding the obstacle between you and your goal, then solving that first.

Simple framework: "I want to achieve X. What's stopping me? That obstacle is my priority."

This prevents the common trap of working hard on the wrong things.

### Pillar 3: Time Management (Opt-Out, Not Opt-In)

Most people use opt-in time management: "I need to do this task. When should I schedule it?"

The opt-out approach flips this: "I've identified a high-priority task. Why can't I do it right now?"

The cascade:
1. Can I do it now? → Do it now.
2. If not now, can I do it in an hour? → Do it in an hour.
3. If not in an hour, this afternoon? → This afternoon.
4. If not this afternoon, tonight? → Tonight.
5. If not tonight, first thing tomorrow morning.

You need a compelling reason to push something into the future. This approach leverages the momentum of decision-making rather than letting tasks drift.

### Pillar 4: Execution (It's a Self-Talk Problem)

You've got clarity, you've prioritized, you know when to do it. But you're still not starting.

This is where most ADHD programs fail—they focus only on execution tactics (Pomodoro timers, etc.) without addressing the first three pillars.

But when execution genuinely is your bottleneck, it's usually a psychological issue. Specifically, self-talk.

Common pattern: Fear of failure leads to procrastination. "If I never start, I never fail. If I never fail, I don't feel bad about myself."

The logical counter:
- If I try and fail, is that the end of the world?
- Can I try again?
- How long would it take to try, fail, learn, and try again?
- Is that total time less than the time I've already spent procrastinating?

Usually, the answer reveals that action—even imperfect action—is faster than avoidance.

## The SpaceX Principle: Intentional Failure

During his time at SpaceX, Ali observed something powerful: engineers there set goals with the intention of learning why they fail.

They're not perfectionists. They try, fail fast, extract lessons, and iterate. This speed of iteration is a core reason SpaceX moves faster than competitors.

Applied to personal productivity: What if you approached tasks with the goal of learning rather than perfecting? What if "failure" was just data for the next attempt?

## Divide and Multiply

With the system in place, the final steps are structural:

**Divide**: Allocate specific times for different aspects of your system. This varies by person—your chronotype, energy patterns, and commitments all factor in. Know yourself.

**Multiply**: Execute consistently. After one to two weeks of following the system, it becomes natural. The initial setup cost (roughly two hours) pays dividends for the rest of your life.

---

## The Outcome

When this system works, you get more than productivity. You get:
- Higher self-esteem from consistent completion
- The ability to self-diagnose when you're stuck (is it a clarity problem? prioritization? time? execution?)
- Mental bandwidth for the things that matter beyond work

The system isn't about becoming a machine. It's about having just enough structure to be effective without feeling suffocated.

Productivity, done right, creates freedom.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Subtract before you add** — Productivity is 90% about removing obstacles, not adding tools
2. **Clarity first** — Know your goal and why it matters before anything else
3. **Prioritize the bottleneck** — Work on what's blocking your goal, not just what's on your list
4. **Opt-out time management** — Ask "why can't I do this now?" instead of scheduling for later
5. **Execution is psychological** — Procrastination usually stems from fear of failure; use logic to counter it
6. **Embrace intentional failure** — Learn fast by trying, failing, and iterating like SpaceX
7. **Consistency beats intensity** — Divide your day, then multiply through daily repetition
