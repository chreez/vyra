## Introduction: The $100B Investment

![Introduction](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-00-15.jpg)

The tech world exploded when **Nvidia (NVDA)**, the world's most valuable company, announced a $100 billion investment into **OpenAI**, the world's most valuable private company. This massive deal linking two titans of the AI age sent valuations skyrocketing across the board.

**Nvidia (NVDA)** stock surged. **OpenAI's** private valuation jumped. Other chip companies saw their values climb. But beneath the celebration, something more complex was brewing—something that left analysts with more questions than answers.

What's really going on under the hood of this $100 billion deal? To understand it, we need to introduce a third player to this game. And to explain them, we start with something surprisingly simple: bicycles.

## Finance 101: Revenue vs Profit

![Finance 101](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-02-50.jpg)

Imagine you run a bicycle shop. You buy bicycles wholesale from a factory and sell them to customers with a markup. This is how you report your earnings:

Your **top line** is revenue—the raw cash you just made. If you sold five bicycles for $20 each, you made $100 in revenue.

Then you subtract all your costs:
- Wages: $40
- Rent: $20
- Bikes from factory: $30

What's left? $10 of profit. This is your **bottom line**.

In the world of investing, you either want to be growing your top line (selling more bikes) or your bottom line (making more profit). You have to be growing one or the other to show growth to Wall Street and get your stock price to go up.

If you're doing badly on both? Your company is not doing all that great.

## Oracle's Mysterious Stock Surge

Enter **Oracle (ORCL)**, now the 15th largest company in the world, led by CEO Larry Ellison. **Oracle** builds the massive data centers that other companies rent out to use for AI.

Five days ago, **Oracle (ORCL)** had a massive stock surge after reporting their earnings. Here's the twist: **they did not beat on the top line and they did not beat on the bottom line.** They had no revenue growth and no profit growth.

And yet somehow their stock went to the moon—up 30%—making Ellison the new world's richest man, passing even Elon Musk.

Why would that happen?

The answer lies in a simple number: their **RPO number**—Remaining Performance Obligations. These are deals they've signed that they haven't been paid for yet. Oracle announced they had signed deals worth **$455 billion** for data centers they're going to build, but haven't built yet.

When they announced this, the market went crazy. "Holy shit, there's so much revenue coming. It's not here yet, but it's coming."

## The Triangle Emerges

![The Triangle](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-06-00.jpg)

Let's connect the dots. We now have three players: **OpenAI**, **Oracle (ORCL)**, and **Nvidia (NVDA)**.

**OpenAI** doesn't build data centers themselves—they rent them from companies like **Oracle (ORCL)**. In fact, **OpenAI** is the one that signed the deal that added $300 billion to **Oracle's** RPO. They're going to give hundreds of billions of dollars to **Oracle** to rent their data centers.

**Oracle**, when they build these data centers, needs to buy the hardware. Where do they go? To **Nvidia (NVDA)**. They're going to give that $300 billion to **Nvidia** to buy GPUs to build the data center.

Now here's the critical question: **Where does OpenAI get the money to pay Oracle to pay Nvidia?**

This is a big problem. **OpenAI** has been searching for ways to finance their trillion-dollar AI plan. They don't have the money now. Sam Altman, when asked about it, said: "There's always a lot of focus on technological innovation. But what really drives progress is when we figure out how to innovate finances, financial models."

He wants financial innovation. Well, let's introduce some.

## The Missing Piece: Where's The Money?

![Where's The Money](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-05-35.jpg)

Here's how the circle closes: **Nvidia (NVDA)** investing $100 billion in **OpenAI**.

Think about it. If **Nvidia** puts money into **OpenAI**, which rents servers from **Oracle (ORCL)**, which builds servers with **Nvidia** chips—you've got yourself a beautiful circle. An infinite money glitch where all three companies can win.

If this pattern reminds you of something suspicious, trust your instincts. Because this exact scheme has happened before, and it didn't end well.

## Historical Echo: The Dotcom Bubble

The year 2001 marked the height of the dotcom bubble. But beyond the infamous failures of startups like pets.com, there was a specific mechanism that extended the bubble's life beyond its natural cycle: **vendor financing**.

A 2002 article dubbed it "the stupid loan bubble." Vendor financing occurred when businesses financed not only their customers' purchase of their products—the switches, routers, and networking equipment—but also funded the growth of those customers' entire businesses.

The analogy: imagine if Ford started loaning money to customers specifically to buy Ford cars.

This practice became one of the most dangerous symptoms of the bubble. Internet hardware makers effectively propped up demand for their own products by lending money to the very companies buying from them. This extended the bubble's lifespan but made the eventual collapse far more severe.

**Why was vendor financing so dangerous?**

It became addictive. Companies could smooth out their revenue growth artificially, creating the appearance of healthy business fundamentals when the underlying economics were fundamentally broken. The loans made everything look good on paper—until the payments stopped coming.

The parallel to today's AI infrastructure boom is striking.

## Nvidia's Growing Portfolio

![Nvidia's Portfolio](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-10-05.jpg)

Beyond the OpenAI loop, **Nvidia (NVDA)** has been quietly building an extensive investment portfolio in AI infrastructure companies. The pattern is consistent: Nvidia finances companies that then use those funds to purchase Nvidia hardware.

### CoreWeave - $6.3B Lifeline

**CoreWeave** operates AI data centers similar to **Oracle (ORCL)**. When the company needed capital, Nvidia provided a **$6.3 billion** investment.

**What CoreWeave did with the money:** Purchased Nvidia's latest Blackwell GPUs at scale.

When the Financial Times questioned this arrangement, CoreWeave downplayed Nvidia's involvement. However, the circular flow of capital is undeniable—Nvidia's money flows to CoreWeave, which immediately flows back to Nvidia through GPU purchases.

### InScale - $1.1B in London

**InScale**, a small London-based startup, raised **$1.1 billion** in an Nvidia-backed funding round.

**Primary use of funds:** Purchasing Nvidia GPUs for data center buildouts.

Nvidia has been minting new AI data center "unicorns" every few weeks using this same playbook.

### The Guarantee Mechanism

Nvidia isn't just providing capital—they're offering **demand guarantees**. If companies build data centers with Nvidia GPUs but can't find customers to rent the capacity, Nvidia backstops the risk.

In the short term, this eliminates downside risk for data center builders. In the long term, it concentrates enormous financial exposure on Nvidia's balance sheet.

Jensen Huang reportedly told one CEO: **"I look forward to making a fortune off of you."**

## The Leakage Problem

![The Leakage Problem](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-12-00.jpg)

Here's an astute observation: doesn't Nvidia lose some money from all those cycles?

Great question. OpenAI has salary costs. Oracle has salary costs. If you give them $100 billion, they're not going to actually have $100 billion left over. It's not one-to-one. You're going to lose a little bit as the circle goes around.

The cost of building a one-gigawatt data center is only about two-thirds GPUs themselves. A lot goes to land, construction, and energy.

Realistically: Nvidia might give OpenAI $100 billion. They spend $20 billion on salaries, and $80 billion goes to Oracle. Oracle spends $20 billion on land and construction, and $60 billion goes back to Nvidia.

**So there's money being lost in this circle. It's not a forever machine.**

That's where you come in.

## You Are The Money Source

![You Are The Money Source](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-13-00.jpg)

You and Wall Street and everybody's 401(k) that automatically buys the biggest companies.

As this machine, as this triangle goes round and round, everyone on the outside gets more and more excited and dumps their money into these growing companies.

Here's the sequence:

1. **Nvidia and OpenAI announce their $100 billion deal**
   → Global chip stock rally. The 401(k)s pile in. Wall Street piles in. You pile in.

2. **OpenAI gets a private market surge**
   → They're hitting $500-600 billion valuation. That's money flowing in.

3. **Oracle shares skyrocket**
   → "Software giant scores massive AI deals with OpenAI"

The same $100 billion is flowing around, but it's causing more and more outside money to flow in from excitement.

**The infinite money glitch comes from you and me and our 401(k)s.** They can circle this around forever while we keep pumping money in. That's the scheme.

## Two Possible Endings

![Two Possible Endings](/blog/the-infinite-money-glitch/00-14-45.jpg)

So how does this end? So far, it's working great. $100 billion might turn to $200 billion next year, $300 billion the year after. They keep moving money around and everyone's happy showing growth.

But there are two ways this can end:

### The Optimistic Ending (Happy Jensen)

If you ask Jensen Huang himself, he'd say this circle will go round and round with more money going in until OpenAI—or Meta, or Microsoft—gets **AGI** (artificial general intelligence).

Once they get AGI, the profits will be insurmountable and it will all pay for itself. If we get AGI within the lifecycle before this whole thing goes belly up, then it's all worth it. OpenAI's profits will be crazy. They can pay Oracle, who can pay Nvidia, and everyone gets paid back.

### The Pessimistic Ending (Sad Jensen)

The dotcom bubble take: this triangle will break once one of these three cannot make their payments. And the most likely candidate is OpenAI.

Once one of these three can no longer afford the hundreds of billions—actually, trillions—they've promised, then suddenly Oracle's earning statements look bad, and they can't afford to buy more chips. And then it all breaks down.

**OpenAI currently doesn't make any money.** Their CEO is willing to run at a loss, but they're losing billions. Eventually, if people stop putting money in, they can't make these payments.

Even Sam Altman admitted: "Somebody is going to lose a phenomenal amount of money when bubbles happen. Smart people get overexcited about a kernel of truth."

There is a kernel of truth—AI is a very powerful technology. But someone somewhere might be getting overexcited about it.

As long as people like Zuck are willing to throw hundreds of billions of dollars and possibly lose it for fear that they'll be too late to AGI, this cycle can keep going. But if that cracks for a moment, this triangle could deflate into something we haven't seen before in human history.

Until then, as long as Zuck's partying, we have to party on like it's 2001.

## What This Means for Your Portfolio

Understanding this circular investment pattern isn't just academic—it has direct implications for personal investors, especially those with 401(k)s and retirement accounts that automatically allocate to large-cap tech stocks.

### The Big Picture

The fundamental question: is this 2001's vendor financing bubble, or 1995's early internet opportunity?

**If AGI arrives soon (3-5 years):** Companies like **Nvidia (NVDA)**, **Oracle (ORCL)**, **Microsoft (MSFT)**, and **Meta (META)** will generate massive returns. Early investors win big.

**If AGI doesn't arrive:** The circular financing breaks down. Companies can't make payments. The triangle collapses. Investors lose heavily.

Most investors don't need to make an all-or-nothing bet. Here are three approaches based on your risk tolerance and market outlook.

### Three Investment Approaches

#### 1. Ride the Wave (High Risk, High Reward)

**The thesis:** If AGI arrives before the payments stop, early investors in NVDA, ORCL, and related AI infrastructure companies will see massive returns.

**How to implement:**

- Direct positions in **Nvidia (NVDA)** and **Oracle (ORCL)**
- Exposure to **Microsoft (MSFT)** and **Meta (META)** - the companies with deep pockets willing to lose billions for fear of missing AGI
- AI-focused ETFs for diversified exposure
- Cloud infrastructure: **Amazon (AMZN)** AWS and **Google (GOOGL)** Cloud

**Risk:** You're essentially betting that AGI arrives within 3-5 years. If it doesn't, the triangle breaks and losses could be catastrophic.

**Who this suits:** Investors with high risk tolerance, long time horizons, and capital they can afford to lose.

#### 2. Sell the Shovels, Not the Gold (Moderate Risk)

**The thesis:** Instead of betting on who wins the AI race, invest in the underlying infrastructure that all players need regardless of outcome.

**How to implement:**

- **Energy companies:** AI data centers consume massive power - infrastructure providers win regardless of AGI timeline
- **Data center REITs:** Real estate plays on the physical locations these facilities require
- **Utility companies:** Power providers in regions with major data center growth
- **Construction/materials:** The physical buildout of gigawatt-scale facilities needs raw materials

**Risk:** Lower than direct tech exposure, but still tied to AI buildout continuing.

**Who this suits:** Investors wanting AI exposure with more downside protection.

#### 3. Hedge Against the Bubble (Capital Preservation)

**The thesis:** This looks like 2001 vendor financing all over again. Protect capital and wait for the correction.

**How to implement:**

- **Reduce exposure:** Lower mega-cap tech allocation in 401(k)s
- **Build cash reserves:** Position to deploy after a potential correction
- **International diversification:** Reduce US tech concentration
- **Defensive sectors:** Consumer staples, healthcare, financials - areas uncorrelated with AI
- **Inverse ETFs:** Speculative bet against tech/semiconductors (advanced strategy, use with extreme caution)

**Risk:** Missing out on substantial gains if AGI does materialize. Opportunity cost of sitting in cash.

**Who this suits:** Conservative investors near retirement, or those who believe the circular financing is unsustainable.

### Key Indicators to Watch

Monitor these signals to gauge which scenario is unfolding:

**Positive Signals (Good news for AI investors):**

These indicate the AGI timeline is accelerating and investments will pay off:

- **OpenAI**, **Anthropic**, or other labs achieve demonstrable AI breakthroughs
- Rapid adoption of AI tools beyond current projections
- Companies showing real return on investment from AI infrastructure spending

**Negative Signals (Warning signs of collapse):**

These indicate the circular financing is breaking down:

- **OpenAI** misses payments or announces down rounds (raising money at lower valuations)
- **Nvidia (NVDA)** guarantee backstops get triggered - they have to buy back excess data center capacity
- **Oracle (ORCL)** RPO numbers drop significantly
- **Microsoft (MSFT)**, **Meta (META)**, or **Google (GOOGL)** executives signal reduced AI spending
- Credit rating agencies downgrade companies in the triangle

### The Pragmatic Approach

For most investors, a balanced strategy makes sense:

**1. Don't fight the trend entirely**

Maintain some exposure to AI leaders through index funds. Complete avoidance means missing out if AGI does arrive.

**2. Don't over-concentrate**

Limit single stock positions in **NVDA**, **ORCL**, or any individual AI company. Diversification protects against company-specific failures.

**3. Diversify across all three strategies**

Mix growth exposure, infrastructure plays, and defensive positioning. This hedges both scenarios.

**4. Rebalance regularly**

If AI stocks grow to 30-40%+ of your portfolio through appreciation, trim back to maintain balance.

**5. Keep cash reserves**

Maintain dry powder to deploy if/when a correction occurs. Cash is optionality.

### The Bottom Line

Whether this is 2001's vendor financing bubble or 1995's internet opportunity depends entirely on the AGI timeline. As an individual investor, you don't have to make a binary bet.

The smartest approach: acknowledge both possibilities, position accordingly, and avoid the extremes of either being entirely out of the biggest wealth creation opportunity in history or being over-concentrated in what might be the biggest bubble since the dotcom era.

Stay informed, stay diversified, and don't let FOMO override sound risk management.
