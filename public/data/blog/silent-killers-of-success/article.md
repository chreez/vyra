## Overview

Most people who fail don't fail because of a lack of talent or opportunity. They fail because of what Jim Rohn calls "attitude diseases" — subtle mental habits that quietly erode everything you're trying to build. Rohn speaks from experience, claiming he suffered from every single one of these diseases before learning to overcome them.

In this lecture, Rohn walks through seven specific attitude diseases: indifference, indecision, doubt, worry, over-caution, pessimism, and complaining. For each one, he diagnoses the problem, explains why it's destructive, and prescribes a cure. The throughline is simple but powerful — your thinking habits, not your working habits, determine the quality of your life.

## Indifference: The Danger of Drifting

The first disease is indifference — the shrug of the shoulder, the person who just drifts through life without real effort or concern.

Rohn's core point: you cannot drift to the top of the mountain. The cure is to pour everything you have into whatever you're currently doing. Not someday when you get a better job, but right now. Wherever you are, go all in.

This does two things. Either it opens up opportunity in your current situation, or it quickly reveals that you should be doing something else. The delusion is thinking you'd try harder if circumstances were better. That's backward — effort comes first.

## Indecision: Mental Paralysis

The second disease is indecision — the inability to make up your mind until it becomes chronic. The person on the fence who's afraid of getting off on the wrong side.

Rohn's advice is blunt: after a while, it doesn't matter which side. Just get off. Pick a direction and go with everything you've got. If it turns out to be wrong, you'll find out quicker — and wrong decisions give you the experience to make better ones.

A life full of adventure is a life full of many decisions. Don't see how many decisions you can avoid. See how many you can get into.

## Doubt: The Plague of Self-Doubt

The third disease is doubt, particularly self-doubt. The person who doubts whether they can do that well, make that much, accomplish that goal. Chronic self-doubt compounds over time and quietly destroys your future.

The cure: flip the coin over and become a believer. Believe in many things, but start with yourself. Rohn frames it simply — understanding your own self-worth is the beginning of progress.

## Worry: The Devastating Habit

The fourth disease is worry, which Rohn calls the most devastating of the group. Worry causes health problems, social problems, personal problems, and family problems. Left unchecked, it can drop you to your knees.

Rohn admits he was once a "super worrier" and that it took him almost a full year to break the habit — one of the toughest years of his life. But once free of it, he discovered you can live an incredible life free of worry. Not free of challenge or difficulty — free of worry. There's a difference.

His advice: give it up. It's not easy, but it's worth it.

## Over-Caution: The Timid Approach to Life

The fifth disease is over-caution — being so afraid of risk that you never take action. Rohn describes it as the language of the poor: "What if this happens? I better not try."

What changed Rohn's perspective was realizing that everything is risky. Getting married is risky. Having children is risky. Going into business is risky. Investing is risky. Life itself is risky — you're not going to get out alive.

The real cost isn't in trying and failing. If you think trying is risky, wait until you get the bill for not trying. If you think investing is risky, wait until you get the tab for not investing.

His prescription: don't ask for security, ask for adventure. Better to live 30 years full of adventure than 100 years safe in the corner.

## Pessimism: Poor Thinking Habits

The sixth disease is pessimism — always looking on the bad side, checking all the reasons why something can't be done. The pessimist doesn't try to figure out what's right; he looks for faults, and when he finds them, he's delighted. This is the person who looks through a window and sees the specs on the window instead of the sunset.

Rohn connects pessimism to a broader principle taught by his mentor, Earl Shoaff: poor thinking habits keep most people poor. Not poor working habits — most people work hard. They just don't think hard.

Shoaff taught Rohn that the mind is like a factory. Whatever you think about all day pours ingredients into that factory, and that's what builds the economic, social, and financial fabric of your life. As you think, so you become.

![Pessimism leads an ugly life](/blog/silent-killers-of-success/pessimism-ugly-life.jpg)

## Guard Your Mind: The Mental Factory

This principle — guarding your mental inputs — gets its own emphasis in the lecture. Rohn offers a test: if someone says they want to be a great leader, the first thing to do is follow them home and check their library. What a person reads pours massive ingredients into their mental factory, and the fabric of their life is built from those ingredients. Fill it with trash, and you can't expect a rich, dynamic life — you might as well try making a cake with cement.

Rohn used to start every morning reading the newspaper, loading up on wars, murders, tragedies, and bad news, then wondering why his days went poorly.

Shoaff's famous instruction: "Every day, stand guard at the door of your mind." You decide what goes into your mental factory, because you have to live with the results. Don't let anyone dump whatever they want into it.

Rohn illustrates this with a story from speaking at a high school in Danbury, Connecticut. He asked the students: what happens if someone drops sugar in your coffee? You're fine. What if someone drops strychnine? You're dead. What if your worst enemy drops in the sugar? Still fine. What if your best friend accidentally drops in the strychnine? Still dead.

The lesson, as Rohn frames it: life is both sugar and strychnine, so watch your coffee. It doesn't matter who hands you the bad stuff or where you get it — it'll still do its damage. Select the right ingredients, keep out the wrong ingredients, and it starts with thought.

## Complaining: A Deadly Disease

The seventh and final disease is complaining — crying, whining, griping. Rohn warns that even slight indulgence in it can destroy your future.

His framing is stark: spend five minutes complaining and you've wasted five minutes. Keep it up and you may have begun what he calls "economic cancer of the bone." The disease will haul you into a financial desert and let you choke on the dust of your own regret.

## Key Takeaways

- **Pour it on now.** Don't wait for better circumstances to give full effort — effort creates circumstances.
- **Decide and move.** Wrong decisions give experience; indecision gives nothing.
- **Become a believer.** Self-worth is the beginning of progress.
- **Give up worry.** It's hard but possible — and the payoff is enormous.
- **Accept risk.** Everything worthwhile is risky; the bigger risk is not trying.
- **Guard your mind.** What you think about determines what you become — choose your inputs carefully.
- **Never complain.** Even five minutes of it is five minutes wasted.
