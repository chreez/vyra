## Overview

Anil Seth opens with a strange fact about anesthesia: unlike sleep, it doesn't just pause awareness — it deletes it. "I could have been under for five minutes, five hours, five years, or even fifty years," he says. That total oblivion frames the central mystery: how does the combined activity of billions of neurons, each a tiny biological machine, generate a conscious experience at all?

Seth's answer reframes perception itself. The brain isn't a passive receiver of the outside world. It's a prediction engine, locked inside a skull with no direct access to light, sound, or objects — only streams of electrical impulses. What we experience is the brain's best guess about the causes of those signals, constantly checked against sensory input.

His provocative reframe: perception is hallucination. Uncontrolled hallucination is what most people mean by the word. Everyday perception is the same process, only reined in by sensory data. "We're all hallucinating all the time," Seth says. "It's just that when we agree about our hallucinations, we call that reality."

## Consciousness Is Not the Same as Intelligence

Seth's research pushes back on a common assumption — that a sufficiently smart machine will eventually wake up. He argues consciousness has less to do with pure intelligence than with our nature as living, breathing organisms.

"You don't have to be smart to suffer, but you probably do have to be alive."

The implication runs through the entire talk: conscious experience is a property of biological systems that must stay alive, not of information-processing systems in the abstract. That's why he considers the prospects for a conscious AI "pretty remote" — even if machines get faster, smarter, and better at conversation, that isn't the axis that produces sentience.

## The Brain as a Prediction Engine

To understand perception, Seth asks you to imagine being a brain. No lights, no sound inside the skull — just electrical signals only indirectly related to whatever is out there. Perception has to be informed guesswork: the brain combines incoming signals with prior expectations to form its best guess of what caused those signals.

Two demonstrations make the mechanism concrete.

**The Adelson checkerboard illusion.** Two patches, A and B, look like different shades of gray. They are the same shade — proven by dropping a gray bar that connects them. The visual cortex has a built-in expectation that cast shadows dim surfaces, so it "corrects" for the shadow and reports B as lighter than it really is. The prediction overrides the raw signal.

**The Brexit audio clip.** Seth plays a garbled recording — it sounds like nonsense. Then he plays the clean version: "I think Brexit is a really terrible idea." He replays the original garble, and now you clearly hear the words. Nothing about the sensory input has changed. What changed is your brain's best guess about the cause of that input, and that changes what you consciously hear.

The lesson: perception depends as much, if not more, on predictions flowing from the inside out as on signals coming from the outside in.

## Perception as Controlled Hallucination

To dramatize what happens when perceptual predictions get too strong, Seth's lab combined virtual reality with an algorithm based on Google's DeepDream. They took panoramic footage of a university campus and seeded the algorithm to predict dogs. The result: a plaza distorted with dog-like features everywhere — a psychedelic playground that looks strikingly like the hallucinations people report in altered states or psychosis.

![Text overlay defining hallucination as uncontrolled perception](/blog/brain-hallucinates-reality/hallucination-definition.jpg)

This is where Seth flips the definition. If hallucination is uncontrolled perception, then ordinary perception is also a kind of hallucination — a controlled one, in which the brain's predictions are reined in by sensory information from the world.

We don't passively perceive reality. We actively generate it, then check the generation against the signals coming in.

## The Self Is Also a Controlled Hallucination

The stranger claim comes next: your experience of being *you* is a controlled hallucination too.

Most of us treat selfhood as unified and continuous — obvious in a way visual illusions never are. Seth argues we shouldn't. There are many distinct ways we experience being a self:

- The experience of having a body and of being a body
- Perceiving the world from a first-person point of view
- Intending to do things and being the cause of things that happen
- Being a continuous, distinctive person over time, built from memory and social interaction

Psychiatrists and neurologists know these components can all come apart. The unified self we take for granted is a fragile construction, another output of the brain that needs explaining like any other.

## The Rubber Hand Illusion

To show the bodily self is also a best-guess construction, Seth walks through a classic experiment simple enough to do at home. A person's real hand is hidden. A rubber hand is placed in front of them. Both hands are simultaneously stroked with a paintbrush while the person watches the fake hand.

![Live demonstration of the rubber hand illusion between two participants at a table](/blog/brain-hallucinates-reality/rubber-hand-illusion.jpg)

After a while, most people report an uncanny sensation: the rubber hand feels like it's part of their body. The congruence between seeing touch and feeling touch, on an object that looks like a hand and sits roughly where a hand should be, gives the brain enough evidence to fold the rubber hand into its best guess of "self."

Even the boundary of your body is a prediction, not a fact.

## Interoception: Feeling Yourself From Within

We don't just experience our bodies as objects viewed from the outside. We also experience them from within — a mode of perception Seth calls **interoception**.

![Anatomical diagram illustrating interoception — perception of internal bodily states](/blog/brain-hallucinates-reality/interoception-diagram.jpg)

Sensory signals from the inside of the body continually inform the brain about the state of internal organs — how the heart is doing, blood pressure, and so on. It's often overlooked because it's rarely conscious, but it's critical: perception and regulation of the internal state of the body is what keeps us alive.

A follow-up experiment from Seth's lab at Sussex shows how tightly this is bound to selfhood. Participants see a virtual-reality version of their hand that flashes red in time — or out of time — with their actual heartbeat. When the flash syncs with the heartbeat, people feel a stronger sense that the virtual hand is part of their body.

Seth also draws a key distinction: internal-body perception isn't like external perception. You don't perceive your kidneys or liver as objects. In fact, you rarely notice internal organs at all unless something goes wrong. That's because interoception isn't about figuring out what's there — it's about control and regulation, keeping physiological variables inside the tight bounds compatible with survival.

When the brain uses predictions to figure out what's there, we perceive objects as causes of sensations. When the brain uses predictions to control and regulate, we experience how well or badly that control is going.

## We Predict Ourselves Into Existence

Pulling the threads together:

- What we consciously see is the brain's best guess of what's out there.
- Our experienced world comes from the inside out, not just the outside in.
- The rubber hand illusion shows this applies to what is and isn't our body.
- Self-related predictions depend critically on sensory signals from deep inside the body.
- Experiences of being an embodied self are more about control and regulation than about figuring out what's there.

Our experiences of the world and of ourselves within it are controlled hallucinations shaped by millions of years of evolution to keep us alive in worlds full of danger and opportunity. "We predict ourselves into existence."

## Three Implications

Seth closes with three consequences of taking this view seriously.

**1. Mental illness is misperception.** Just as we can misperceive the world, we can misperceive ourselves when the mechanisms of prediction go wrong. That opens new opportunities in psychiatry and neurology — targeting mechanisms rather than just treating symptoms in conditions like depression and schizophrenia.

**2. You cannot be uploaded.** What it means to be you cannot be reduced to or uploaded into a software program running on a robot, however smart. We are biological flesh-and-blood animals whose conscious experiences are shaped at every level by the biological mechanisms that keep us alive. Making computers smarter is not going to make them sentient.

**3. Human consciousness is one point in a vast space.** Our individual inner universe is just one possible way of being conscious. Human consciousness generally is a tiny region in a vast space of possible consciousnesses, all grounded in biological mechanisms shared with many other living creatures.

## Key Takeaways

- Perception is not passive reception — it's the brain's best guess about the causes of sensory signals, generated from the inside out.
- Everyday perception and hallucination are the same process; hallucination is just perception uncorrected by sensory data.
- Illusions like the Adelson checkerboard and the "Brexit" audio clip show that prior expectations can override or reshape raw sensory input.
- The self is not a single thing but a bundle of separable components — bodily ownership, first-person view, agency, continuity — any of which can come apart.
- The rubber hand illusion demonstrates that even the boundary of your own body is a prediction the brain can be tricked into updating.
- Interoception — perception of the body's internal state — grounds selfhood in biology and is oriented toward regulation and survival, not object recognition.
- Consciousness is a biological phenomenon, not a byproduct of intelligence. Getting smarter doesn't get you closer to sentience.
- Understanding perception as controlled hallucination reframes mental illness as a mechanism problem, not just a symptom problem.
