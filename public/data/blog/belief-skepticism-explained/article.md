## Overview

You knew exactly what to say. You rehearsed it in the shower, word for word. But when the moment came — when your boss named that number, when your father asked that question in that tone — something went flat. Your mouth agreed. Your hands went cold. And an hour later on the drive home, the perfect sentence arrived, perfectly useless.

You call that nerves. Vadim Zeland, a Russian author trained in academic science, calls it something stranger: you were asleep. Not tired — asleep inside a waking dream, eyes open, reading lines from a script someone else wrote for you.

His 2024 book *Transurfing Yourself* is a follow-up to the *Reality Transurfing* series. It opens with a confession: the old method worked flawlessly. A few pages later comes the warning. All your knowledge is worth nothing if you stay in an unconscious dream. This book teaches you to be awake when it counts.

## What Sleep Actually Means

Zeland's model is simple. You have two screens. The outer one holds the room, the faces, the phone. The inner one holds your thoughts, replays, and rehearsed arguments. Your attention almost always sits glued to one of them.

Glued to the outer screen, you forget yourself and react on autopilot. Lost in the inner one, you miss what's in front of you. Either way, nobody is home. A freeze is the extreme case — when one screen takes all of you at once.

Waking up isn't a mystical event. It's a small move of attention. You pull it off both screens, put it in the middle, and ask where it is and what it's doing. From that center, you can see the room and your thoughts at the same time. Zeland's line for this: *your attention is yourself as you are, without clothing.*

His rule for the whole practice: any absurdity in waking life should alert you. A colleague's tone doesn't fit the words. A worry grows about something that hasn't happened. Your own voice agrees to something you don't want. If even the absurd things don't wake you, he says, you need to do something about it.

## Markers: The Code Words That Knock You Out

Old films sometimes use a plot where someone is hypnotized and programmed to respond to a code word. Someone says the word, and the person goes blank and executes the program. Zeland uses that image for markers.

A marker is a negative belief that binds you and puts you to sleep. *You are wrong. You should. You are guilty. You are weak.* When someone triggers it, you drop into the dream on cue — unable to respond the way you would have liked.

Reader markers Zeland collected were painfully specific:
- *What will people think?*
- *I'm too late for everything.*
- *If you come in second, that's a complete failure.*
- One reader's mother used to say: *Who needs you except me?*

None of these are thoughts anyone chose. Someone placed them, or a defeat did.

![Write down the sentence that freezes you](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/write-down-the-sentence.jpg)

**Elena's story makes this concrete.** She's 38 and has run the accounts nobody else wants for six years. Tuesday at four, she has the meeting she asked for. The figures are printed. She practiced the sentence in the car: *I'm asking for a raise that matches the work.*

Her manager smiles, leans back, and says: *Let's be realistic here.*

Elena is nine again at the kitchen table, asking her father for new shoes. He said: *Don't be greedy. Let's be realistic.* The same words, the same tone. Her shoulders rise. Her voice gets small. She hears herself say: *Of course I understand.* The printed figures stay in the folder.

On the drive home, the right sentence arrives, clear and unanswerable. One hour too late. Elena wasn't weak in that room. She was asleep. *Let's be realistic* was the code word.

Zeland's instruction: let your markers become your activators. The same sentence that knocks you out becomes your alarm clock. Every time it fires, it's a signal to wake up.

When you replace the old attitude — *Should I? I know I am free. Can't I? I know I can do it. Am I weak? No, strength is in me* — write down the sentence that freezes you. Under it, write the answer you'd give if you were awake. That second line is what you say next time.

It also helps to remember who you were before the markers arrived. Zeland grew up in Kamchatka where winter meant digging snow tunnels and jumping off roofs into snowdrifts. Decades later he asked whether he could get that body and that feeling back. The answer came, he writes, as if from an angry overseer: *You can, idiot. But why did you have to waste it all?*

His exercise: find one day in your past when nothing could touch you — where you were, what you did, how it felt in your body. He calls it your basic settings. If you can't find one, build a new one from where you stand. Either way, that state is what you're waking up into.

## How to Wake Up: The Four-Stage Method

Zeland breaks the wake-up into four stages:

1. **React to the activator.** The moment something tenses you, notice where your attention went.
2. **Ask questions.** *What am I imagining right now? Am I building a mountain? Am I afraid of something that hasn't happened?* Children have this habit, Zeland says. Adults have lost it.
3. **Disagree.** In a dream, what keeps you asleep is that you accept everything as given. So refuse it: *This does not suit me.*
4. **Set your own reality.** That's where the braid comes in.

## The Mountain That Isn't There

Zeland retells a parable about snails who live in a town with a mountain at its center. Every morning they disappear to climb it. Every evening they return exhausted. When a visitor asks why, they say: *Because that's how it's supposed to be.* The visitor tells them they don't need to climb it. The snails can't believe it. *Is that even possible?* From then on, life became easy.

The mountain is made of self-importance. You decide a goal is hard — it needs money you don't have, skills you don't have, luck you've never had. That's the mountain. Then you climb it with all your might and call the exhaustion effort.

![Snails climbing the mountain they invented](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/snails-climbing-mountain.jpg)

The difference from earlier Zeland: before, the instruction was to lower importance and calm down. Here he goes further. Don't lower the mountain — notice that it isn't there. *First remove the mountain, and then go to the kiosk for a newspaper. The goal was a stroll all along. You turned it into a battle.*

His thought form: *There is no mountain. It's only in my head. I walk across the plane and calmly take what's mine.*

The diagnostic: if your goal is taking forever to arrive, you've probably imagined it as a high mountain and you're still climbing.

But saying *there is no mountain* is hard when everything in you is braced. So Zeland gives a release first — the *I Can Do Anything* exhale. Breathe out hard through your nose and imagine a blast wave leaving your body, strongest at the shoulders. Say *I am free* or *I can do anything.* Then from that lighter state, say: *There is no mountain.* The blast comes first, then the sentence. The release makes room; the sentence moves into that space before the old weight settles back.

## The Braid of Intention

Zeland says humans carry an archaic vestige called the braid of intention — an energetic focal point behind the back between the shoulder blades. You can't see it, but you can sense it the way people sense a limb that was once there. It can be restored, trained, and developed.

When the braid activates, it lifts slightly toward the back and its tip touches a point about an elbow's length behind the shoulder blades, which Zeland calls the assemblage point.

A simple exercise: exhale and imagine the air leaving straight through your back between the shoulder blades. Something rises there. Practice calling it up on the exhale and letting it go. If you feel nothing, Zeland has a fix that sounds like a joke: *clip a clothespin to your shirt between your shoulder blades.* You'll feel that. Keep part of your attention on that spot as if you were holding the clothespin there with your mind; use the rest to set what you want.

His point: energy follows attention, whether you feel it or not. A braid can be replaced with attention. The clothespin just gives your attention somewhere to stand.

![Energy follows attention](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/energy-follows-attention.jpg)

**The full move — highlighting the target frame:**
1. Enter presence. Notice where your attention is and take it back.
2. Activate the braid. While you hold that feeling, set the frame in thoughts, words, or images.
3. Let the feeling go.

A target frame is the end result as you'd see it — the signed contract, the quiet kitchen. It's never the story of how you get there.

**What the braid cannot do:**
- Set what has already happened
- Single-handedly set a shared reality (a major project, the situation in a country)
- Set a script — only a frame
- Set a specific person's behavior

What it can do: set a meeting with someone, set a relationship with a person you haven't named, set that people in general treat you warmly. The distinction is frames vs. scripts — you set the outcome, not the other person's lines.

This is also why you need to be awake when you aim it. One reader got caught up in jealousy on her first flight ever and held the thought: *I want everyone to look only at me.* Soon everyone was looking — her face had swollen bright red from sunburn. The braid worked exactly as aimed. She had just aimed it while asleep.

## Energy and Body as Foundation

There's a catch in the mountain technique. To accept that the mountain doesn't exist, you need a clear mind and a sufficiently high energy level. When your head is overloaded and your energy is at zero, the mountain looks solid no matter what you tell it.

*Note: The narrator of the original video is an AI and explicitly says: "I don't eat — don't take my word for any of what follows, and don't take his either." Zeland himself writes that cleansing is not his specialization and that you should research these questions yourself. He also warns against strict diet systems. Treat this section as one man's practice, not a prescription, and check anything here with someone qualified before changing what you eat.*

Zeland's principle: the body and mind clean themselves if you stop clogging them. Less junk food, fewer junk sources of information — your call what counts as junk. His five basics: healthy eating, healthy movement, healthy sleep, clean water, and a clean mind. Go to bed at the same time every night. Block the feeds that pour negativity into you.

He pairs the body with the braid in one small ritual: take a contrast shower, alternating hot and cold. When you turn off the water, close your eyes and inhale. On a strong exhale, open them sharply and activate the braid in the same moment. Say clearly: *I am pure, powerful, awakened.*

Every ritual in this chapter is a scheduled wake-up. The shower, the meal, bedtime — moments you'd otherwise sleepwalk through. Zeland puts an alarm clock inside each one.

## Going Ahead: Training on the Boring Ones

Here's the real problem, and Zeland says it plainly: you know how to wake up but you don't know when. You won't remember at the decisive moment because the decisive moment is exactly what puts you to sleep.

So you train on easy ones first. He gives three gloriously boring examples: the gas station, the ATM, the checkout.

![Alarms in ordinary places](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/alarms-in-ordinary-places-title.jpg)

Before you put the fuel nozzle in the tank: *Where am I? What am I about to do? Which pump? Which fuel?* At the ATM: *I see the wallet, I see the card, I see myself.* At the checkout, one step further: *I see myself, I see my wallet, I see the cashier. Does she see me? No — she's asleep.*

When you're truly present, Zeland says, sleepers notice something without knowing what. They're drawn to you without knowing why. You're like a firefly in a realm of shadows. His advice: keep it quiet. Don't advertise your presence. Watch reality move without gripping it by the throat.

**Two rules that make the habit stick:**
- If you catch yourself expecting something from reality, don't wait for it. Ask for the reality you need and set the frame right there.
- On days when you feel bad and nothing works, don't set reality at all. Just loosen your grip: *My world is coming into balance. I'm resting and my world will take care of everything.*

One question cuts through every habit and desire you carry: *Does it possess me, or do I possess it?* A craving that owns you is a line from someone else's script. A choice you own is yours.

## Notebooks, Notes, and Daily Anchors

You'll forget all of this by tomorrow morning. Zeland knows it. So the night before, write a note that says *I will remember* and put it next to your bed. In the morning you wake up once, see the note, and wake up again.

Then he adds two notebooks:
- **Morning notebook:** write a page of declarations — what you want as finished results, aimed forward.
- **Evening notebook:** write a page of statements — every small thing that moved toward it today.

Morning sets the frame. Evening confirms it. You can repeat the same pages day after day. What matters is that your attention lands where you chose, twice a day.

His reason: a saying from Russian cosmonauts who found that memory in orbit turns surprisingly short. *The dullest pencil is better than the sharpest memory.* Zeland thinks the rest of us are living in orbit now, too.

## The Spirit Access Point

Scan the space behind your back with your attention. Zeland says you'll find two spots. The first is the one you know — an elbow's length behind the shoulder blades. The second sits behind and above the back of your head at roughly 45 degrees, about half a meter out. He calls it the access point to the spirit.

His map: the body and mind are your avatar. Behind and above it is your spirit, which was always there and simply went unvisited. The access point is where you knock.

![Your attention is you](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/your-attention-is-you.jpg)

A simple exercise: stand up, tilt your head slightly down, and let your gaze fall to the floor as you exhale — as if a beam ran from your eyes to a spot ahead of your feet. Now inhale and draw that beam back into your eyes, lifting your head to look straight ahead. Hold for two seconds. Notice that you weren't thinking anything. Your attention closed on itself. That's the first step of returning to yourself.

From there, lift that same attention back and up to the point above the back of your head. Use a beam, an arrow, a cloud, or the braid itself — it doesn't matter. Only the contact matters. In that moment, Zeland says, you stop observing your attention and become it. His phrase: *I am attention itself.*

Readers' reports range from a quiet reset to calm certainty to something one called "god mode." Zeland's own instructions are less dramatic: enter the state briefly, many times a day. Set a frame from there if you want. Then let it go. The effect is cumulative — each visit leaves a little more of it behind.

Experiment: before you speak to someone, enter the state and keep it while you talk. A person you talk to from there, Zeland says, subconsciously feels your power.

## The Full Loop

Every road in this book ends in the same place. Presence, the braid, and the spirit point are all moves of attention.

Run this the next time you feel yourself going flat:

1. The activator fires and something tightens.
2. Ask where your attention is and take it back.
3. Ask what you're imagining and whether it's a mountain.
4. Exhale, say *I can do anything*, then say *there is no mountain*.
5. Find the braid (or the clothespin), set the target frame, and let it go.

**Applied to a real situation** — a hard conversation tomorrow about rent, a raise, or a boundary:

- Tonight: write *I will remember* and put it by your bed. Write your marker on a card — the exact sentence that freezes you — with your awake answer underneath.
- Morning notebook: write the frame. The conversation already over, and you calm.
- When the code word comes, it's your alarm. In the room, skip the rituals. Find the spot behind your shoulder blades. One exhale. *There is no mountain.* Then say your awake answer slowly.
- That evening: open the second notebook and write what actually happened — even if the only change is that you noticed the moment you started to fall.

You will fail at first. You'll wake up after the meeting, not during it. That still counts. Each late wake-up pulls the next one a little earlier, until one day it arrives inside the room.

## Why It Works Even If You Don't Believe It

![Cue, action, follow-through](/blog/belief-skepticism-explained/cue-action-followthrough.jpg)

Peter Gollwitzer's research on if-then plans shows that tying an action to a specific cue makes people significantly more likely to follow through. That's what an activator is — a cue that triggers a planned response instead of an automatic one.

Sian Beilock's research on choking shows that pressure floods attention with worry and self-monitoring. Naming the marker, moving attention to a physical point, and shrinking the stakes are all ways of taking that attention back.

The clothespin is the cleverest part. It splits your attention on purpose: one part stays anchored in your body, one part holds the outcome. So a single sentence from across the table can't take all of it. That's what freezing is — one sentence getting all of you. Zeland's method makes sure some of you stays behind.

## Key Takeaways

- Sleep, in Zeland's terms, is losing self-awareness and running on autopilot. Waking up is a move of attention, not willpower.
- Markers are old negative beliefs that act like code words — when triggered, you freeze. Find your marker and make it your alarm.
- The four-stage method: notice the activator, ask questions, disagree, set your own reality.
- The mountain is built from self-importance — inflate a goal and you exhaust yourself climbing it. Notice it isn't there.
- The braid (or clothespin) splits your attention so no single trigger can take all of it.
- Train the wake-up at boring moments: gas station, ATM, checkout. It has to be automatic before it works at the decisive moment.
- Morning and evening notebooks anchor your attention to what you chose, twice daily.
- The spirit access point is an advanced step — attention directed above and behind the head, described as returning to yourself.
