## Overview

Most people assume artisan sourdough requires expensive equipment and professional training. The reality is simpler: flour, water, salt, a sourdough starter, and your hands. That's everything you need to produce bread that surpasses nearly anything available at a grocery store.

This guide follows a complete home-scale sourdough bake from start to finish, drawing on the methods used at Proof Bread, an artisan bakery in Phoenix, Arizona. The baker behind Proof Bread recently moved from a garage operation to a commercial bakery on Main Street, but returned to the garage workspace specifically to demonstrate that the same principles scale down to a home kitchen without compromise.

Hand mixing is not only practical for home bakers — it's educational. The tactile feedback from working dough by hand teaches you things a stand mixer never will. You'll feel gluten develop, sense hydration levels, and build an intuitive relationship with your dough that improves every time you bake.

## The Four Ingredients

A country-style sourdough loaf requires just four things:

- **Flour** — A blend of white bread flour and whole grain flour works well. Proof Bread uses a custom "Proof Blend" that's roughly 50/50 white bread flour and Type 85 stone-milled heritage grain flour from Hayden Flour Mills, a local Arizona mill. The white flour provides strong gluten structure (some bakers call it "scaffolding"), while the whole grain flour contributes flavor and nutrition from the bran and germ.
- **Water** — About 570 grams for two loaves. Temperature matters enormously here.
- **Sourdough starter** — A mature, active culture at or near peak fermentation. About 170 grams for this batch.
- **Salt** — 21 grams (roughly 2.5% of the flour weight).

## Why Hand Mixing Works

Hand mixing is about as efficient as a stand mixer for batches up to around 60 loaves. Below that threshold — and especially for a home batch of one to four loaves — there's little reason to use a machine.

The advantage of hand mixing is the feedback loop. You feel how the dough transforms from shaggy and loose to smooth and extensible. You notice flour pockets that need incorporating. You develop a sensitivity to hydration and gluten development that transfers to every future bake.

The technique is straightforward: grab the bowl with one hand, and use the other hand like a spatula-scoop. Scrape the edges, dive into the middle, pull at the dough, bring it back to center. Find a smooth, rhythmic motion — roughly the length of three songs, or 10 to 15 minutes of continuous mixing.

## The Autolyse: Let Time Do the Work

The autolyse is a rest period after combining just flour and water — no starter or salt yet. Mix until there are no dry flour clumps larger than about a centimeter, then cover the bowl and let it rest for a minimum of 20 minutes.

During this rest, enzymes released from the flour begin breaking down starches, and gluten bonds form passively. The result is dramatic: dough that would rip apart immediately after mixing develops noticeable stretchiness after just 20 minutes of sitting untouched.

This passive development is the core principle of sourdough baking. You can develop dough mechanically (by force) or through a combination of time and gentle handling. The latter produces better bread — long-fermented sourdough developed gently is significantly easier for the body to digest.

The minimum autolyse is 20 minutes, but you can extend it to two hours if your schedule requires it. Longer is fine. Place the bowl in a warm spot near the stove or on top of an appliance generating heat.

## Water Temperature: The Most Common Mistake

Mixing too cold is one of the easiest mistakes to make. For a small home batch, the dough loses heat rapidly to the surrounding environment — far more than a large commercial batch would.

Start with water around 112°F (44°C). This sounds high, but by the time you combine it with room-temperature flour, add cold starter, and hand-mix for 10-15 minutes, the final dough temperature will drop significantly. The target is a final dough temperature near 84°F (29°C), which is the ideal balance for both wild yeast and lactobacillus bacteria activity.

In this demonstration, the water started at 112°F, the autolyse dough measured 92-93°F, and after mixing in the cold (59°F) starter and 15 minutes of hand work, the final dough landed at 77°F. That's workable — the warm spot above the stove helped maintain temperature through the bulk fermentation.

The key principle: every temperature around you is trying to cool your dough. Compensate with warm water up front, and use a warm spot throughout the process.

## Baker's Percentages: Building Your Formula

Baker's percentages express every ingredient as a proportion of the total flour weight. Flour is always 100%, and everything else is relative to it.

**Hydration** = water ÷ flour. A country loaf typically lands in the low-to-mid 70s percent range.

The critical detail with sourdough is that your starter contains both flour and water. A 1:1 starter (equal parts flour and water by weight) means 200 grams of starter adds 100 grams of flour and 100 grams of water to your totals.

**Example formula for two loaves:**

| Ingredient | Weight | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|
| Flour (in autolyse) | 850g | — |
| Water (in autolyse) | 570g | — |
| Sourdough starter (1:1) | 170g | ~20% of flour |
| Salt | 21g | 2.5% of flour |
| **Total flour** | **935g** | **100%** |
| **Total water** | **655g** | **~70%** |

## Understanding Your Sourdough Starter

Every sourdough culture follows the same lifecycle: an exponential growth phase, a period of stasis at peak, and then a decline as nutrients are exhausted and cells begin to die.

**When to mix:** Use your starter at or near peak — the top of the curve where the microbial colony is at maximum activity. A starter slightly past peak and beginning to recede is still usable, but you'll get the best results from a robust, actively rising culture.

**Feeding schedule for bake day:**

1. Pull starter from the fridge one day before you plan to mix
2. Feed it at a 1:2 ratio (100g mature starter + 200g flour + 200g water)
3. Target 84°F after feeding, then leave at room temperature for at least 5 hours — 8 hours is typically where it reaches the edge of stasis, a good time to feed again
4. Feed a second time that evening
5. Let it go 4-5 hours at room temperature, then refrigerate before bed
6. Mix with it the next morning — it'll be mature and ready

Two feedings before mixing produces a significantly more robust starter than one feeding or none. The fridge is a preservation method, not a life-giver — the culture is slowly declining in cold storage. Each feeding triggers a new exponential growth cycle that strengthens the colony.

## Completing the Mix

After the autolyse, add the sourdough starter and salt on top of the dough. Get your hands wet (water acts as a barrier between skin and dough) and begin dimpling the starter into the dough with a cat-like kneading action.

A practical tip: weigh the salt separately onto a lid or small plate. It's difficult to hit exactly 21 grams when pouring directly into a mass of dough, and you can't scoop salt back out once it's in. Over-salting to 5% would ruin the batch.

As you work, keep your hands wet — this is your bassinage. The extra water (about 30 grams total for this batch) gets incorporated gradually through wet hands rather than being dumped in all at once. Each time you pause, re-wet your hands before continuing.

Once the starter and salt are roughly incorporated, start the main mixing rhythm. Scoop underneath, stretch upward, let gravity pull the dough back. Rotate the bowl continuously. Use slightly open fingers to feel for any remaining clumps and smooth them out.

The mix is done when the dough passes a near-windowpane test — you can stretch it thin enough to see light through it without it tearing immediately. This takes roughly 10-15 minutes of rhythmic hand work after the autolyse.

![Dough showing visible gluten development](/blog/artisan-sourdough-hand-mix/gluten-development-dough.jpg)

## Stretch and Fold Sets

After mixing, the dough rests in a warm spot and receives three sets of stretch-and-folds spaced at least 30 minutes apart. Each set takes about one minute.

**Technique:** Wet your hands and wrists. Loosen the dough from the bowl's edges. Scoop underneath one side, stretch it upward as far as it will go without tearing, then fold it into the center. Rotate the bowl 90 degrees and repeat, working your way around. After completing all sides, flip the dough over to lock in the folds.

Each successive set feels different. The dough becomes stretchier, more elastic, and easier to tension. You're adding exponential layers with each fold — stretching out gluten strands, building tension, then letting the dough relax so you can come back and add more.

The minimum 30-minute rest between folds gives the gluten time to relax and new bonds to form passively. Don't take the timing too literally from any recipe — it varies with temperature and flour type. The goal is building as much strength as possible before shaping.

![Stretch and fold technique on floured surface](/blog/artisan-sourdough-hand-mix/stretch-and-fold-technique.jpg)

## Reading Your Dough on the Fermentation Curve

Before shaping, assess where your dough sits on the fermentation curve. Look for gas bubble buildup on the surface, volume increase, and a lively, airy feel when you handle it.

If the dough is still in its exponential rise phase (visible activity but not yet at stasis), you have two options:

1. **Shape now and leave at room temperature for an hour** before refrigerating — extending the bulk fermentation after shaping
2. **Wait another hour** for the dough to reach closer to peak, then shape and refrigerate immediately

If the dough has already reached stasis, go straight to shaping and refrigeration to prevent overproofing. An overproofed dough has exhausted its energy — it won't spring in the oven and will bake flat.

The ability to read fermentation comes with repetition. Every bake teaches you something. Take notes on your observations so you can build on previous experience rather than starting from scratch each time.

## Shaping: Triangle Fold and Tuck-and-Roll

Turn the dough out onto a lightly floured wooden surface (wood holds temperature better than cold granite or metal). Divide the dough — for two loaves, cut roughly in half.

**Pre-shaping (optional):** Round each piece by tucking the edges underneath to build surface tension. Let it rest 5-10 minutes. Pre-shaping is helpful when working with multiple pieces or shaggy dough, but you can skip it if the dough already has good surface tension.

**Shaping technique:**

1. Dust the top with flour and flip the dough over
2. Stretch it into a triangle shape
3. Fold the top edge over toward the center
4. Grab the top corners, stretch them out, and fold each side two-thirds across (like a letter fold)
5. Grab the bottom, stretch it up to meet the first fold line, then overlap the sides
6. Tuck and roll toward yourself, building tension with each roll by pressing down with thumbs and pulling back with fingertips
7. Rotate 90 degrees and tuck the ends in

The finished shape should hold its form — it shouldn't droop or flatten when you pick it up.

![Dusting flour on dough before shaping](/blog/artisan-sourdough-hand-mix/dusting-flour-for-shaping.jpg)

## Into the Banneton and Cold Retard

Dust the shaped loaf with rice flour (not wheat flour — rice flour doesn't absorb moisture, so it prevents sticking without incorporating into the dough). Scoop the loaf with a bench knife, rice-flour side down, and place it seam-side up into the banneton.

Cover the banneton with a produce bag or plastic wrap to prevent the fridge from drying out the surface. A dry outer layer leads to uneven baking.

The cold retard (overnight refrigeration) serves two purposes:

1. **Scheduling flexibility** — shape in the evening, bake fresh in the morning
2. **Better scoring** — cold dough holds its shape under the blade, producing cleaner cuts and better ears

The tradeoff: ambient-proofed loaves tend to have slightly more volume and a more open crumb, since cooling slows fermentation. Cold-retarded loaves score more crisply and have a tighter, more even crumb.

![Banneton proofing baskets lined with parchment](/blog/artisan-sourdough-hand-mix/proofing-baskets-bannetons.jpg)

## Baking in a Dutch Oven

A preheated Dutch oven replicates the steam injection of a commercial deck oven using nothing but cast iron and the loaf's own moisture.

**Baking procedure:**

1. Preheat the Dutch oven inside your home oven at 500°F (260°C). The high starting temperature compensates for heat loss when you open the oven door
2. Turn the loaf out of the banneton onto the hot cast iron
3. Score with a lame (razor blade) — the cold-retarded loaf will score more crisply
4. Cover with the lid and bake for 20 minutes (steam phase — the trapped moisture creates oven spring and a glossy crust)
5. Remove the lid, reduce temperature to 440°F (225°C), and bake another 10-15 minutes for crust color development

The loaf is done when the internal temperature reaches 205°F (96°C) or higher, and it sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom.

## Reading the Crumb

Cut the cooled loaf in half to assess your fermentation. The crumb is a historical record of the baker's work.

**What good fermentation looks like:** Uniformly distributed small-to-medium holes expanded to roughly the same size throughout. The crumb should be airy and open enough that you can see translucent spots when sliced thin, but not so open that spreads fall through.

**Signs of under-fermentation:** Giant holes next to dense, unexpanded pockets. This means fermentation sites weren't evenly established or the bulk ferment was cut short.

The tighter you shape and the more you fold, the more evenly distributed the fermentation sites will be. An extremely open crumb (popular in some baking circles) is a different goal — Proof Bread aims for an even, moderately open crumb that balances aesthetics with practicality.

## Storing and Refreshing Bread

Store sourdough in a linen bag or bread box at room temperature. The crust will soften over a few days, but the interior remains good for four to five days.

To refresh an older loaf: brush the crust liberally with water until it looks as shiny as a fresh-baked loaf, then place it in a hot oven for about 10 minutes. This restores the eggshell-crisp crust one final time. It works once — after that, the loaf is best used for toast or croutons.

Allow a freshly baked loaf to cool for at least two to three hours before slicing. The bread is still curing during this time, and the full spectrum of flavor doesn't develop until it's completely cooled. Cutting it hot produces a single, undifferentiated texture and flavor.

## Building a Baking Rhythm Into Your Life

Sourdough baking is a series of short active interventions separated by long passive periods where microbes do the work. 
**A typical home rhythm:**

- **Evening:** Pull starter from fridge, feed it. Go about your evening.
- **Morning:** Mix autolyse (5 minutes). Go make breakfast.
- **Mid-morning:** Complete the mix with starter and salt (15 minutes). Set aside.
- **Through the day:** Three sets of stretch-and-folds (1 minute each, 30+ minutes apart)
- **Afternoon/evening:** Shape, place in banneton, rest at room temperature, then refrigerate
- **Next morning:** Preheat oven and Dutch oven, bake (35 minutes total)

The baker describes this as "stacked interventions" — the same principle that runs a commercial bakery. You're the alchemist guiding natural processes, intervening briefly to strengthen and direct the dough, then stepping back while millions of microbes do the heavy lifting.

If you make more than you need, give a loaf away. Freshly baked sourdough is something that truly cannot be bought at a grocery store, and even a beginner's home-baked loaf will likely surpass most commercial bread.

## Key Takeaways

- Hand mixing is practical and educational for home-scale batches — no stand mixer required
- The autolyse (20+ minutes of flour and water resting) does most of the gluten development passively
- Start with warm water (~112°F) to counteract ambient cooling — target 84°F final dough temperature
- Feed your starter at least twice before mixing for a robust, active culture
- Three sets of stretch-and-folds, spaced 30+ minutes apart, build strength gently through time
- Shape when fermentation is active but not yet past peak — learn to read the curve through repeated baking
- A Dutch oven at 500°F replicates commercial steam injection: 20 minutes lidded, then 10-15 uncovered
- Even, uniformly distributed holes in the crumb indicate successful long fermentation
- Let the loaf cool 2-3 hours before slicing for full flavor development
- Bake often, take notes, and build an intuitive relationship with your dough
