# The Honeymoon Method: ADHD-Friendly Routines That Actually Work

## Overview

If you've ever tried to follow a "perfect morning routine" from a YouTube video or Instagram influencer, only to abandon it after a day or two, you're not alone. For people with ADHD, autism, depression, or anxiety, traditional routines are fundamentally incompatible with how our brains work.

The core problem is this: conventional routines assume you wake up with the same energy and motivation every day. But ADHD brains live in a dopamine deficit. Some mornings you wake up energized and ready to tackle everything. Other mornings, the thought of brushing your teeth feels overwhelming. When the only routine you have is the "perfect" one, you're set up to fail on every day that isn't perfect.

The Honeymoon Method solves this by creating **three versions of the same routine**—Ideal, Most-Likely, and Minimum—each calibrated to different energy states. This approach has transformed how hundreds of neurodivergent people maintain consistent routines without the guilt spiral of "failure."

### Why "Honeymoon"?

The name references the "honeymoon phase"—that initial burst of excitement and motivation when you start something new. You know the feeling: you watch an inspiring routine video, feel pumped, execute perfectly for a day or two... then the honeymoon ends. Traditional routines only work during that honeymoon energy. This method acknowledges that honeymoon-level motivation is temporary and plans for the days when it's gone.

## Why Traditional Routines Fail ADHD Brains

### The Dopamine Deficit Problem

ADHD brains are hardwired to seek dopamine. When you wake up in a low-energy state—feeling unmotivated, maybe a little depressed, not excited about the day's tasks—your brain will find dopamine somewhere. Without a realistic routine option, that usually means scrolling your phone in bed until 10 minutes before work.

This isn't a character flaw. It's neurochemistry. Your brain needs motivation, dopamine, and interest to initiate tasks. On low-energy days, a 15-step morning routine simply won't generate enough dopamine to compete with the instant gratification of your phone.

### The All-or-Nothing Trap

The real damage happens after you "fail" your routine. You internalize it as evidence that you're broken, that routines "aren't for you." This creates a vicious cycle:

1. See an influencer's perfect routine
2. Feel motivated, try it for 1-2 days
3. Have a low-energy day, can't complete it
4. Feel guilty and conclude routines don't work for you
5. Abandon all structure
6. Feel worse without any routine
7. Repeat

The insight that breaks this cycle: **the routine isn't the problem—having only ONE version of it is.**

## The Honeymoon Method: Three Tiers of the Same Routine

The Honeymoon Method creates three versions of your routine, each designed for a different energy state. Crucially, these aren't three different routines—they're the same routine at different intensity levels.

### Tier 1: The Ideal Routine (High Energy Days)

Start by imagining your best possible day. You woke up energized, motivated, with time to spare. What would your dream morning look like?

Write down everything you'd want to accomplish. This is your aspirational target—not your daily expectation.

**Example Ideal Morning Routine:**
1. Wake up, go to bathroom (phone charges there overnight)
2. Brush teeth, wash face, complete bathroom tasks
3. Walk dog for 45-60 minutes
4. Cook a nutritious breakfast (actual cooking, not just grabbing something)
5. Have coffee or tea mindfully
6. Do full hair and makeup, choose an outfit that feels good
7. Journal—reflect on thoughts or plan intentions
8. Create a detailed to-do list for the day
9. Process emails before starting other work

**Key insight:** You will NOT do this routine most days. That's not failure—that's expected. The Ideal routine exists for the days when you have the energy to match your aspirations.

![Ideal routine discussion](/blog/adhd-routine-guide/ideal-routine.jpg)

### Tier 2: The Most-Likely Routine (Average Energy Days)

Now take your Ideal routine and ask: "What's realistic on a normal day when I feel fine but not exceptional?"

This is probably your most common state. Not depressed, not euphoric—just average. Adjust each element down to match.

**Example Most-Likely Morning Routine:**
1. Wake up, bathroom, grab phone
2. Brush teeth, wash face
3. Walk dog for 20-30 minutes (not 45-60)
4. Make a simple breakfast—avocado toast instead of bacon and eggs
5. Coffee or tea
6. Get ready—do hair and makeup but with less effort (ponytail instead of styling, quick makeup instead of full routine)
7. Skip journaling
8. Skip detailed to-do list
9. Skip morning emails

The same activities appear, but with realistic time and effort adjustments. You're still maintaining the routine's structure while acknowledging your actual capacity.

![Most-likely routine breakdown](/blog/adhd-routine-guide/most-likely-routine.jpg)

### Tier 3: The Minimum Routine (Low Energy Days)

This is the crucial tier. On days when you wake up feeling terrible—unmotivated, potentially depressed, wanting to stay in bed—what's the absolute minimum you can do while still maintaining your routine habit?

**Example Minimum Morning Routine:**
1. Go to bathroom, do basic tasks (you're already there)
2. Walk dog around the short loop (10 minutes) OR let dog out in backyard
3. Eat something—microwave meal, cereal, anything quick
4. Getting ready is optional—stay in pajamas or put hair in ponytail with a hat

**The minimum baseline should be: feed yourself.** If all you accomplish is eating something—even if it's DoorDash delivered to your bed—that's a win. That's your routine completed for the day.

This reframe is transformative: instead of "I failed my routine," it becomes "I completed my minimum routine." The habit stays intact. The guilt disappears.

## Why This Works for Neurodivergent Brains

### Satisfies Competing Needs

For people who are both autistic and have ADHD, there's an internal conflict:
- **Autistic brain:** Craves structure, predictability, and routine
- **ADHD brain:** Requires flexibility, novelty, and freedom from rigid expectations

The Honeymoon Method satisfies both. You have the structure of a consistent routine framework (same activities each tier) while maintaining flexibility in how much you do based on your actual state.

### Eliminates Guilt

When your only routine is the Ideal one, every day you can't complete it feels like failure. With three tiers, completing your Minimum routine on a hard day is **success**. You worked with your energy level, not against it.

### Maintains the Habit

Consistency research shows that maintaining any version of a habit is better than breaking the streak entirely. By having a Minimum tier that's achievable even on your worst days, you never fully break your routine habit. The neural pathways stay active.

## How to Build Your Own Honeymoon Method Routine

### Step 1: Dream Big (Ideal)

Write out your fantasy routine assuming unlimited energy and motivation. Include everything you'd love to do in an ideal world. Don't self-censor.

### Step 2: Get Realistic (Most-Likely)

Go through each item and ask: "What's the 70% version of this?" Shorter walk, simpler breakfast, less elaborate grooming. This should feel achievable on a regular Tuesday.

### Step 3: Find Your Floor (Minimum)

For each remaining item, ask: "What's the bare minimum that still counts?" Some items may disappear entirely. That's fine. Your minimum should be achievable even when you feel like doing nothing.

**Pro tip for the Minimum tier:** If your minimum still feels overwhelming, cut it down further. For some people, the minimum routine is literally: eat something. That's it. Start there and protect that baseline fiercely.

### Step 4: Apply to Evening Routines Too

The Honeymoon Method works for any routine. Build out Ideal, Most-Likely, and Minimum versions for your evening wind-down as well.

## Where to Display Your Routine

Once you've built your three tiers, put them somewhere you'll actually see them. The key is visibility at the moment of decision—when you wake up and need to choose which tier fits today.

**High-visibility options:**

- **Bathroom mirror** — You're going there anyway. A simple card with all three tiers keeps the choice front and center
- **Phone lock screen** — Create a wallpaper with your three tiers. You'll see it before you can start doom-scrolling
- **Nightstand card** — A laminated index card you flip to each morning. Physical objects work better for some ADHD brains than digital reminders
- **Notion/notes widget** — If digital works for you, pin it to your home screen where it's impossible to miss

**Format suggestions:**

- **Side-by-side columns** — Ideal | Most-Likely | Minimum, so you can scan and pick
- **Traffic light colors** — Green (Ideal), Yellow (Most-Likely), Red (Minimum) for instant visual recognition
- **Checkbox format** — Some people like checking off items even on minimum days

**The goal:** Remove friction from the morning decision. You shouldn't have to remember your tiers—you should see them automatically when you need them.

## Key Takeaways

- **Traditional routines assume consistent energy levels**—ADHD brains don't have that
- **Create three tiers of the same routine:** Ideal (high energy), Most-Likely (average), Minimum (low energy)
- **Your Minimum baseline should be simple enough to complete on your worst day**—even just eating something counts
- **Completing your Minimum routine is success, not failure**—you maintained the habit
- **This approach satisfies both structure-seeking and flexibility-needing parts of neurodivergent brains**
- **The goal is consistency across energy states, not perfection on ideal days**

## Common Questions

**Q: Won't I just always do the minimum?**
A: In practice, no. When you have more energy, you naturally want to do more. The tiers give you permission to do less when needed—they don't prevent you from doing more when able.

**Q: How do I know which tier to use?**
A: Check in with yourself when you wake up. How's your energy? Your motivation? Your mood? The answer is usually obvious. Trust your self-assessment.

**Q: What if my minimum is too minimal?**
A: There's no such thing. If your minimum is eating a granola bar, that's valid. The point is maintaining any version of the routine, not achieving some external standard.
