## Overview

Most people try to get rich by chasing money — grinding harder, hunting the next opportunity, forcing results. Hudson Cosper argues this approach is backwards. Money is not something you pursue; it's something you attract. And what you attract is determined entirely by who you believe yourself to be.

Your income is a reflection of your identity. If you hold the self-image of someone who doesn't make $10K a month, it's not just unlikely that you'll earn it — Cosper argues it's functionally impossible. The mind that believes itself undeserving of wealth will sabotage every attempt to reach it, through procrastination, hesitation, and self-doubt. The fix isn't more hustle. It's a new identity.

The good news: identity is not fixed. It's performed, then believed. This video lays out exactly how to make that shift.

## Why Chasing Money Doesn't Work

Money is energy. When you focus on chasing it, you're operating from a place of lack — which only reinforces the belief that money is scarce and hard to get. Cosper's reframe: instead of asking "how do I get more money," ask "who do I need to become so that money comes to me?"

The person who attracts money isn't trying to catch it. They move with the confidence and standards of someone who already has it. The world — and your subconscious — responds to that signal.

## Your Income Reflects Who You Believe You Are

Procrastination is not a discipline problem. It's an identity problem. If your self-image is "someone who has never made real money," your mind will resist action toward goals that feel inconsistent with that image. You'll delay, doubt, and drift — not from laziness, but because the action doesn't match who you believe you are.

The same logic runs in reverse: if your identity is "someone who makes $100K a month," it becomes difficult *not* to take action. The behavior flows naturally from the belief.

## Step 1: Stop Identifying With Your Past

Past failures only mean one thing: at that time, you didn't hold the identity of someone who could succeed. That's it. The past is not a prediction.

Cosper's analogy: if you got cheated on in every relationship you've ever had, would you swear off love forever? Probably not. But somehow, when it comes to money, people let past failures define what's possible for them going forward. That's the trap — and the first step out is simply refusing to let the past identity be the present one.

## Step 2: Define and Embody the Future Version of You

Write down your financial goal. Then answer: how does that version of me think? How fast do they make decisions? What habits do they have? What would they never tolerate?

Seeing it on paper gives you a blueprint to perform. You're not pretending to be rich — you're rehearsing the behaviors of someone who is, until your subconscious accepts them as real.

This matters because the subconscious can't tell the difference between performance and reality. It observes your actions and builds your self-concept from them. Act like a millionaire consistently, and eventually your subconscious simply *believes* you are one. At that point, the limiting beliefs begin to dissolve — not through willpower, but through accumulated evidence.

## Breaking Programmed Limiting Beliefs

Most limiting beliefs aren't chosen. They're inherited — from parents, peers, school, and culture. Cosper's dad used to tell him "getting rich isn't a real thing, people just get lucky." That belief, unexamined, would have made wealth impossible. You can't outwork a belief that says the goal isn't even real.

The solution is awareness. You first have to notice the belief exists. Then you trace it: where did it come from? Is it actually true, or did someone who never got rich tell you it was? Only then can you consciously replace it.

Stop taking financial advice from people who aren't where you want to be.

## Standards and Ego as Identity Anchors

Your standards define your identity in practice. If you say you want to be wealthy but sleep until 1pm and avoid hard decisions, your standards are broadcasting a different identity — and your subconscious is listening.

Cosper reframes ego as a tool. The word "ego" literally means the conscious and subconscious self coming together — it's your identity. A strong ego, in this context, means a strong self-concept: "I don't do that. That's beneath who I am." That internal standard stops bad habits before they start, not through willpower but through identity protection.

## The Chain: Identity → Thoughts → Beliefs → Action

This is the sequence Cosper lays out explicitly:

1. **Identity** — who you believe yourself to be
2. **Thoughts** — which ideas feel available and natural to you
3. **Beliefs** — what you're convinced is possible
4. **Action** — what you actually do

Most people try to fix the action without touching the identity. It doesn't work. If the identity says "I'm not someone who makes real money," every action toward wealth feels foreign, exhausting, or pointless. Change the identity first, and the rest of the chain aligns behind it.

## Key Takeaways

- Identity precedes income — your earnings reflect who you believe yourself to be
- Procrastination is an identity problem, not a discipline problem
- Stop letting past financial failures define who you are now
- Write down your goal; describe how that version of you thinks, acts, and decides — then perform it daily
- The subconscious cannot distinguish consistent performance from reality; use this
- Ego (true self-concept) is a feature, not a flaw — use it to hold standards
- Break limiting beliefs by tracing where they came from, not just by pushing through them
- Sequence: identity → thoughts → beliefs → action — work from the top down
