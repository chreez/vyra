## Overview

The law of attraction, the way it's usually sold — vision boards, affirmations, picturing the money until you can almost smell it — doesn't work. When psychologists put that habit under a microscope, the people who practiced it hardest tended to end up worse off.

And yet more than 500 years ago, a yogi named Svātmārāma wrote down a technique a child could learn in one minute: sit in a dark room, stare at a small flame, don't blink. Then he added a strange instruction. *Keep this secret. Guard it like a chest of gold.*

Why would anyone hide a candle? The video traces the answer through seven "secrets" — a surgeon in Manchester, a lawyer who invented a guru, a soldier's discharge papers, three decades of psychology research, and a deadly sweat lodge in Arizona. The thread connecting them: narrowing attention to a single point is a power. Who holds the point decides whether it builds your mind or captures it.

## Secret One: The Instruction Nobody Reads Twice

![The Hatha Yoga Pradipika](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/hatha-yoga-pradipika.jpg)

The source is the *Hatha Yoga Pradipika*, a 15th-century founding manual of physical yoga. If you picture a gentle text about stretching and breathing, its second chapter will correct you. That's where Svātmārāma lists the **Shatkarmas**, the six cleansing acts — swallowing a long strip of wet cloth and pulling it back up, drawing water into the bowels. These aren't relaxation exercises. They're purges.

**Trataka** is on that list.

- **Verse 31** gives the whole method in one line: with a steady mind, gaze at a small point without blinking until tears come.
- **Verse 32** says what it does: it destroys diseases of the eye and drives out sloth and fatigue — and it should be kept hidden, treasured like a box of gold.

Two centuries later, the *Gheranda Samhita* repeated the technique and went further, promising something close to clairvoyance.

Notice what the instruction *doesn't* say. It doesn't say relax. It doesn't say picture your dream life. It says **hold** — and gives a precise way to know you've held long enough: when the eyes start to water.

### The Part the Manual Leaves Out

![Attention drifting away](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/tangled-attention.jpg)

Try it for 60 seconds. Somewhere around the 20th second, you leave. You're replaying something someone said on Tuesday, drafting a reply, wondering if the candle smells strange. The flame is still there. You aren't.

The unsettling part isn't that you drifted — it's that you never *decided* to. You only discover, seconds later, that you've been gone. **That moment of discovery is the entire technique.**

In 2008, Antoine Lutz, Heleen Slagter, John Dunne, and Richard Davidson published a paper in *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* that took focused-attention practice apart like a watch. Trataka belongs to exactly this family — one object held on purpose. What they found wasn't a single skill but a loop:

1. A monitoring faculty that **detects** attention has slipped
2. The ability to **release** whatever grabbed it
3. The ability to **return** attention to the object

Holding still was never the skill. **The return is the rep.**

Four years later at Emory University, Wendy Hasenkamp put meditators in a scanner, had them rest attention on the breath, and asked them to press a button the instant they realized they'd wandered. While minds drifted, the **default mode network** was busy — the system that spins stories, rehearses arguments, replays the past. But the moment of catching it — that small internal jolt of *wait, where am I?* — lit up the **salience network**, the circuitry that decides what deserves to matter right now. Only after that did executive systems drag attention home.

So there's a specific, measurable instant when you realize your mind has been taken. Most people have never trained it once. And that does something ugly to the law of attraction: **you don't move toward what you want — you move toward whatever you keep returning to.** The worry, the person, the screen, the old wound. If you never trained the return, you aren't the one deciding where you go back.

## Secret Two: The Same Light in a Different Hand

In November 1841, a Swiss performer named Charles Lafontaine was touring Britain with a stage show of animal magnetism — mesmerism. He claimed an invisible fluid flowed from his body into his subjects, putting them into a trance. On the 13th, he performed at the Manchester Athenaeum.

In the audience sat **James Braid**, a Scottish surgeon with a busy practice and little patience for magic. He came to see how the trick was done. He didn't find one. At a later performance, he watched a man on stage who couldn't open his own eyelids no matter how hard he tried. Braid came away convinced of two things: *the fluid is nonsense, and the effect is real.*

So he tested it himself. On Monday the 22nd, in his own house before witnesses, he sat a young man named Walker in a chair and had him stare at the top of a wine bottle placed above his line of sight, so his eyes had to strain upward. No hand passes, no magnet, no performer — just a fixed stare at one small bright point. Within minutes, Walker's eyelids trembled, closed, and he went under.

In 1843, Braid published *Neurypnology* and gave the world a new word: **hypnotism**. He first thought he'd found a nervous sleep caused by exhausting the eyes. Later he changed his mind and described the state as **monoideism** — the mind collapsed around a single idea. What mattered was never the eyes. It was the narrowing.

Put the two scenes side by side. A yogi alone in a dark room, eyes fixed on a flame. A young man in a Manchester chair, eyes fixed on the neck of a bottle. The same act. The first produces a person with more command over their own mind. The second produces a person who will raise an arm because a stranger says it's floating. **The difference was never the light. It was the hand holding it.**

### Absorption Is a Door

In 1974, Auke Tellegen and Gilbert Atkinson at the University of Minnesota measured a trait they called **absorption** — the capacity to sink so completely into a film, a song, or a sunset that the rest of the world disappears. They compared it with hypnotic susceptibility. The people most capable of total absorption were the most easily hypnotized.

The ability to lose yourself in something and the ability to be steered by someone aren't opposites. They grow from the same root. Your talent for getting lost isn't a strength or a weakness — it's a door, and a door has no opinion about who walks through it.

![A small bright screen in a dark room](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/glowing-screen-at-night.jpg)

Think about where your eyes go every night: a small, bright rectangle at roughly arm's length in a dim room. You barely blink. You don't look away for a long time. In posture and mechanics, **you're already doing Trataka — for hours. You just don't own the flame.** Someone else chooses what burns inside it, and the reflex they're training isn't the return. It's the absence of one.

## Secret Three: The Guru Who Was Never Born

In 1900, **William Walker Atkinson**, a successful Pennsylvania attorney, collapsed from overwork — "nervous prostration" — and lost his money with his health. He resurfaced in Chicago as a patient of the **Chicago School of Psychology**, run by Herbert Parkyn. It was the first American institution founded to teach suggestive therapeutics — treating people with hypnotic suggestion instead of medicine. It published a magazine called *Suggestion*, which had earlier gone by a more honest name: *The Hypnotic Magazine*.

Atkinson didn't just recover there. He joined. Within months he was writing for the magazine; by early 1901 he was its associate editor. Then came his own magazine for the growing New Thought movement, and in 1906, a book with a familiar title: *Thought Vibration, or The Law of Attraction in the Thought World*.

**The phrase on a million vision boards was made famous by a man who learned his craft inside a hypnosis school.**

It gets stranger. From 1903 on, Atkinson also published books on yoga — *Hatha Yoga*, *The Science of Breath*, *Lessons in Raja Yoga* — signed as **Yogi Ramacharaka**, supposedly an Indian master whose teachings came through a disciple named Baba Bharata. No historical record shows either ever existed. Over about 30 years, Atkinson produced something like a hundred books under names like Theron Q. Dumont, Swami Panchadasi, and Magus Incognito. He's widely believed to be one of the anonymous "Three Initiates" behind *The Kybalion*, still sold today as ancient hermetic wisdom.

So at the dawn of the 20th century, one man was writing America's idea of yoga *and* its idea of the law of attraction — same pen, same years, out of a building that taught people how to put other people under. And something got lost in the crossing:

- **The yogi's discipline was about the eye:** one point — hold it, lose it, catch the loss, return.
- **Atkinson's discipline was about the image:** the thing you want, pictured vividly, felt as if already yours.

The training of *where your attention goes* was quietly swapped for a pleasant fantasy about *where you'd like to end up*.

The line kept running. Parkyn's school taught autosuggestion from the turn of the century. In the 1920s, French pharmacist Émile Coué, who learned his trade among the hypnotists of Nancy, turned it into an international craze. In 1937, Napoleon Hill gave autosuggestion its own chapter in *Think and Grow Rich*. In 1944, Neville Goddard published *Feeling Is the Secret*. In 2006 came the film *The Secret*. A century of teachers, the same move every time: **keep the trance, throw away the flame.**

## Secret Four: The Paperwork

**Neville Goddard** was born in Barbados in 1905, came to New York as a teenager to dance and act, and met a teacher he only ever called Abdullah. By the 1940s he was filling lecture halls with one idea: *imagine the wish already fulfilled, feel it as real, and the world will rearrange itself to match.*

His method had a precise shape. Lie down at the edge of sleep, drowsy and heavy. Hold a single scene that implies the wish is already granted — not the wish itself, but a small moment that could only happen afterward. Live inside that scene again and again until it feels real. Strip away the mysticism and it's a recognizable instruction: **narrow the mind to one image at the hour it's most suggestible.** Braid would have known exactly what he was looking at.

![An army bunk at night](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/army-bunk.jpg)

His favorite proof was his own. Drafted in late 1942 and shipped to Camp Polk, Louisiana, he applied for a discharge; his colonel stamped it disapproved. That night, as Neville told it, he lay in his bunk and imagined himself in his own bed in New York, hearing the city outside. He did it night after night. Then the colonel called him in and approved the discharge.

Then someone pulled his military record. Inducted November 12, 1942; honorably discharged at Camp Polk on March 15, 1943 — "for the convenience of the government." He was **over 38 years old** and released to take employment in essential war industry. Neville was born February 19, 1905. He'd turned 38 three and a half weeks before he walked out. And in his own lecture, he mentions almost in passing that his captain remarked he was *almost 38*. The clue sat inside the miracle the whole time.

### Neither the Believer Nor the Cynic

The easy move is to sneer: he lied or fooled himself, so the method is fiction. The video calls that the cynic's exit — and says it throws away the only part worth keeping.

A frightened man in a war he didn't choose took one image — one room, one bed, one city — and returned to it night after night instead of spiraling into catastrophe. **Imagining didn't move the colonel. The calendar did.** But a mind that keeps returning to a single point stays oriented. It doesn't dissolve into panic. It stays aimed at the exit long enough to be standing there when the door opens.

The believer and the cynic are wrong in exactly the same way. **The power was never in the picture. It was in the return — and the industry sold the picture.**

Before filing this under other people's gullibility, the video asks you to take out your own version: the job that arrived right after you decided, the message that came the week you let go, the parking space. You tell it with a glow in your voice and have never once checked its paperwork — because checking would feel like betraying the person who needed it to be true.

## Secret Five: The Picture Is a Painkiller

![Studying how people imagine the future](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/fantasy-study.jpg)

**Gabriele Oettingen**, a German psychologist at New York University and the University of Hamburg, has spent three decades studying what happens inside people who daydream about the future.

**The weight-loss study (1991, with Thomas Wadden).** She followed 25 women entering a year-long weight-loss program and measured two things beforehand:

- **Expectations** — a sober judgment of their odds. These behaved as you'd hope: women who judged success likely did better.
- **Fantasies** — what they spontaneously imagined at the program's end. These ran the *opposite* direction. Women with rosy, effortless daydreams (slim, admired, transformed) lost *less* weight. Women whose imagined futures still included the struggle — the temptation, the dessert at the party — did better.

The vision predicted the opposite of the vision.

**The emotional bill (2016, with Doris Mayer and Sam Portnow, *Psychological Science*).** Undergraduates imagined 12 open-ended future scenarios and rated how positive their fantasies were. The most glowing fantasizers reported *fewer* depressive symptoms at the time — and *more* a month later. She found the same shape in fourth and fifth graders: fewer symptoms at first, more seven months on. The studies were correlational, and she says so plainly — but the curve kept repeating: **pleasure now, pain later.**

That isn't how a magnet behaves. It's how a painkiller behaves. The picture takes the ache of wanting something you don't have and turns the volume down. But that ache was the engine — the thing that would have made you send the email, leave the job, push away the second plate.

### The Rule That Outlaws the Antidote

Every teacher in that century-long line says some version of: *watch your thoughts, don't dwell on the negative, don't picture what could go wrong or you'll attract it.* Set that beside Oettingen's women. The ones who succeeded were exactly the ones who let themselves picture the temptation, the slip, the hard part.

The law of attraction doesn't just hand you the sedative — **it outlaws the antidote.** It teaches you that looking at your obstacle is spiritual sabotage, so you flinch away from the one image that could have saved you, and call the flinch faith.

And who profits from a painkiller you take every day? A customer who reaches the goal stops buying. A customer who feels perpetually close comes back for the next book, seminar, retreat. The most valuable follower is the one who stays exactly one visualization away from the life they want — forever.

## Secret Six: Whose Flame They Were Staring Into

**James Arthur Ray** was one of the teachers featured in *The Secret*. The film made him a star; he appeared on Oprah and Larry King. In October 2009, more than 50 people paid up to $10,000 each for his five-day "Spiritual Warrior" program at a retreat center outside Sedona, Arizona — days of intense exercises, a 36-hour fasting vision quest alone in the desert, and on October 8, a sweat lodge Ray conducted himself.

He'd told them on a recording it would be "hellacious," that they'd feel like they were dying — but only metaphorically. Inside, it went on round after round in the dark and heat. Survivors described people vomiting, collapsing, lying barely responsive while others pressed on. Eighteen were hospitalized. **Kirby Brown, 38, and James Shore, 40, died that day. Liz Neuman, 49, died nine days later without waking.** In June 2011, a jury convicted Ray of three counts of negligent homicide; he was sentenced to two years in prison. In a 2016 documentary, he said the event *had to happen* — part of how he was meant to learn and grow.

![A closed system of attention](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/closed-system.jpg)

That's what a closed system of attention does: nothing that happens can ever count as evidence against it.

Everyone asked why they didn't leave. The exit was a flap of canvas; nothing physical held them in. The genre Ray came from rests on one premise — *your limits live in your mind; discomfort is the old self resisting; the strong push through.* For days, those people had fixed their attention on his frame. Take Braid's monoideism and seal it in a dark, scorching dome. **When attention is welded to someone else's idea, your own body's alarm stops arriving as information. It arrives as interference** — something the brave ones override.

That's real concentration, held by the wrong hand. You'll probably never sit in a sweat lodge, but you've likely stayed in a room after your body told you to leave: the job where the knot in your stomach was rebranded as not being a team player, the relationship where your unease got called insecurity until you called it that too. The signal came, it was accurate, and it arrived as noise — because your eyes were fixed on someone else's flame.

## Secret Seven: The Practice They Wrapped in Gold

The method most modern teachers follow comes from the Bihar School of Yoga, set down by Swami Satyananda Saraswati in *Asana Pranayama Mudra Bandha*. It's embarrassingly simple — which is precisely why it was so easy to hide.

![Sitting with a candle](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/hero.jpg)

**How to practice Trataka:**

1. **Setup:** A dark, still room. A candle at about arm's length, flame level with your eyes — not above them the way Braid placed his bottle.
2. **Gaze:** Sit upright. Look at the tip of the wick — the small dark point inside the flame, not the glow around it.
3. **Hold:** Try not to blink. Your eyes will sting and water. Let them — that's the end point the old text named.
4. **Close:** After a minute or two, close your eyes. The flame remains as an afterimage glowing behind your eyelids.
5. **Return:** Hold the afterimage. It will drift, fade, or change color. Each time it slides off center, **notice, and bring it back.**
6. **Repeat:** When it's gone, open your eyes and begin again. A few rounds — 10 minutes at most when starting.

**Two warnings, and they aren't optional:**

- **Use a candle, never the sun.** Staring at the sun can permanently burn the retina.
- **If you have epilepsy or any eye condition,** skip the flickering flame — gaze at a black dot on white paper instead, and talk to a doctor first.

The practice isn't the flame. It's the moment the afterimage slips and you catch it — Hasenkamp's moment, the salience network waking up. Every catch is one rep of the muscle the people selling your attention need you to never build.

### Taking the Catch Outside

![Manipulation works by narrowing](/blog/tratak-attention-law-of-attraction/narrowing-eye.jpg)

Take that muscle outside the dark room and you'll notice something about manipulation: **almost all of it works by narrowing.**

- The salesman's deadline that shrinks your options to one
- The guilt that makes one person's disappointment the only thing in view
- The outrage that turns one enemy into the only object on the screen

Each is someone placing a small bright point in front of your eyes and waiting for the rest of your field to go dark. The trained catch is the half-second where you feel the narrowing happen and ask two questions: **Why is there only one thing in front of me right now? And who put it there?**

### What the Research Shows

In small studies, honestly reported — yes, it shows up:

- **2014, *International Journal of Yoga*** — Talwadkar, Jagannathan, and Raghuram had 60 elderly people; one group practiced Trataka for a month while another waited. The Trataka group improved on sustained attention and mental flexibility (letter cancellation, the Trail Making Test) and later on digit-span memory.
- **2021, Bengaluru** — Swathi, Bhat, and Saoji at a yoga university trained 41 young adults for two weeks. A single 20-minute Trataka session then beat a session of eye exercises on the Corsi block test, a measure of spatial working memory.
- **2007, Davidson's lab** — Julie Brefczynski-Lewis found practitioners with ~19,000 hours of concentration practice showed *more* activation in the brain's attention network than beginners — but those with ~44,000 hours showed *less*. Their focus had become effortless: attention that holds without gripping.

Not miracles. Measurable.

## The Law of Attraction That Actually Moves a Life

When the flame is done, your eyes are closed, and your mind is steady, do what Oettingen found works where fantasy fails: **mental contrasting.** Picture the wish vividly. Then, in the same steady mind, picture the obstacle — the real one, usually inside you: the habit, the fear, the thing you do at 11 at night. She later built this into a four-step sequence: **Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.**

Fantasy alone sedates. Fantasy held against the obstacle mobilizes. But almost nobody can hold the obstacle — the mind slides off it the way an eye slides off a bright light, straight back to the pleasant picture. **That slide is exactly what the flame trains away.**

That's the connection the industry missed. Concentration is what lets you look at the thing in your way without looking away. The yogis trained the eye; the salesmen sold the picture. You need the eye to survive the picture.

So why would a yogi hide a candle? Not because staring at a flame is magic — because of what it builds. A person who chooses what holds their attention, and who notices within seconds when something else has taken it, is a terrible subject: hard to hypnotize cheaply, hard to sell the same dream twice, hard to keep inside a dark tent after the body says leave. And Svātmārāma's strangest detail finally makes sense. He didn't file Trataka with the gentle practices. He filed it with the purges.

## Key Takeaways

- **The return is the rep.** Focus isn't holding still — it's detecting drift, releasing what grabbed you, and coming back. Most people never train the moment of noticing.
- **You move toward what you keep returning to,** not toward what you want. If you don't train the return, something else chooses it.
- **Narrowed attention is neutral power.** The same single-point focus produced yogic self-command and Braid's hypnotism. The difference is who holds the point.
- **Your phone is an untrained Trataka.** A bright point in a dim room, barely blinking, for hours — with someone else choosing the flame.
- **The modern law of attraction descends from hypnosis.** Atkinson popularized the phrase after training inside a suggestive-therapeutics school, swapping attention training for pleasant fantasy.
- **Positive fantasy works like a painkiller.** Oettingen's research links rosy daydreams to less effort and later distress; including obstacles predicted better results.
- **Manipulation works by narrowing.** When only one thing is in front of you, ask why — and who put it there.
- **Practice safely:** candle at eye level, arm's length, short sessions; never the sun; use a black dot if you have epilepsy or eye conditions.
- **Pair the flame with mental contrasting (WOOP):** a steady mind lets you hold the obstacle instead of sliding back to the fantasy.
