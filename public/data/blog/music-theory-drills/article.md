## Overview

Most piano instruction stops at the same three exercises: chords, scales, and arpeggios. According to experienced piano teacher Pur Pasteur, this covers roughly 2% of music theory. The remaining 98% separates amateur players from those who can actually compose and improvise.

This guide presents a progressive system of 20 drills designed to build complete harmonic fluency. The first five drills establish fundamentals that any neighborhood piano teacher would cover. The remaining fifteen take you into composer territory: voice leading, chord progressions, cycle of fifths, secondary dominants, and modal interchange.

The key insight is that music theory consists of just four or five interconnected concepts. The real challenge is developing fluency across all 12 keys. On guitar, transposing is as simple as moving up a fret. On piano, every key has a visually distinct geometry that must be internalized separately.

## Foundation Drills (1-5)

These five exercises build the essential vocabulary you'll use throughout the system.

### Drill 1: Chromatic Major Chords

Before working within a single key, familiarize yourself with the shape of every major chord. Play through all 12 major chords chromatically, ascending and descending.

A useful insight for black-key chords: they're mirror inversions of nearby white-key chords. B major has an "edgy" structure; B-flat major is its mirror. A major looks like a mountain; A-flat major looks like a valley. Once you see this pattern, building chords on black keys becomes intuitive.

### Drill 2: Tonic Chord Inversions

Pick a key (G major works well because it includes black keys without being overwhelming). Take your tonic chord and practice all three inversions: root position, first inversion, second inversion.

Do this with both hands across the keyboard. The goal is instant recognition of each inversion shape.

### Drill 3: Pentascale

The pentascale is underrated. Most players jump straight to full scales, but the pentascale (five notes: 1-2-3-4-5) uses all five fingers equally. In normal scales, the pinky barely participates except on the final note.

Split the pentascale into upper (3-4-5) and lower (1-2-3) sections. Practice hands together—this forces your weaker fingers (3, 4, 5) to keep pace with the stronger ones.

### Drill 4: Full Scale

Expand to the complete eight-note scale, alternating between groups of three and four fingers. The fingering varies by key, but the principle remains constant.

Already at this stage, think like a composer. Linear scales are boring music. Use your scale as a pool of notes and improvise within it—stay stepwise but vary direction. Don't obsess over fingering; let your body develop intuition. You'll stumble onto beautiful melodies this way.

### Drill 5: Arpeggios

Arpeggios use only the triad notes (1-3-5) expanded across octaves. Basic fingering is 1-2-3, though black-key arpeggios require adjustments to keep the thumb on white keys.

Try the "broken arpeggio": skip one note, come back. Skip one, come back. This creates a pleasing melodic pattern.

## Beyond the Basics (6-8)

These drills introduce variations that develop keyboard geometry and pattern recognition.

### Drill 6: Contrary Motion Scale

Start with both thumbs on the tonic (G). Hands move in opposite directions—one ascending, one descending. The fingering is mirrored between hands, which actually makes coordination easier.

The catch: sharps and flats aren't mirrored. This forces you to think carefully about the geometry of your chosen key.

### Drill 7: Scale in Tenths

Play the scale with your right hand starting a tenth (an octave plus a third) above your left hand. The right hand gets a "head start" of two scale degrees.

This creates a canon effect between the voices. It's excellent for Christmas music and produces rich harmonies naturally.

### Drill 8: Pattern Transposition (Hanon Variations)

The famous Hanon exercise uses a specific interval pattern (+2, +1, +1, +1, -1, -1, -1) transposed up each scale degree. But this is just one of infinite possibilities.

Create your own patterns. Try "shimmering twilight" (ascending diagonals), or invent something new. The exercise teaches you to transpose any melodic fragment through the scale—a skill that appears constantly in real music.

## Chord Harmony (9-11)

Now we stack scale tones into chords and learn to connect them smoothly.

### Drill 9: Diatonic Chord Degrees

Build a triad on each scale degree by stacking thirds. In any major key, the pattern is always: Major, minor, minor, Major, Major, minor, diminished.

![Chord Degrees](/blog/music-theory-drills/chord-degrees.jpg)

Say each chord name aloud as you play: "G major, A minor, B minor, C major, D major, E minor, F-sharp diminished." Add a bass note in your left hand to simulate real piano playing.

### Drill 10: Inverted Chord Degrees

Repeat Drill 9, but start each chord in first inversion (third in the bass). Then try second inversion. This reveals a crucial insight: as you move through inverted degrees, you're essentially moving one note at a time through the scale.

Keep naming chords aloud. You're building mental associations between the chord name (E minor) and its various keyboard shapes.

### Drill 11: The Oscillation (I-IV-V)

The tonic (I), subdominant (IV), and dominant (V) are the most fundamental chord relationships. Practice oscillating: I-IV-I-V-I.

Apply voice leading: when moving between chords, keep common tones in the same voice. Moving from G to C? Keep the G (common tone) in the same finger and move the other notes minimally. This principle—move as little as possible—is the foundation of smooth chord progressions.

Try the minor version: vi-ii-vi-iii-vi (E minor, A minor, E minor, B minor, E minor in G major).

## Progression Mastery (12-14)

These drills teach the chord sequences that appear throughout Western music.

### Drill 12: Descending Thirds with Voice Leading

Play: I-vi-IV-ii-V-I (G-Em-C-Am-D-G). At each step, identify common tones and keep them stationary. The other notes move by step.

This teaches you to see the connections between chords rather than treating each as an isolated shape.

### Drill 13: Cycle of Fifths

Start on the tonic and leap down by fifths: G-C-F#-B-E-A-D-G. Each chord is the "dominant" of the next, creating that characteristic pull toward resolution.

![Cycle of Fifths](/blog/music-theory-drills/cycle-of-fifths.jpg)

With proper voice leading, you get a descending stepwise melody in the top voice—a hallmark of Bach. Think of the cycle as four pairs of V-I motions, each pair "pretending" to resolve before the actual final cadence.

### Drill 14: Pachelbel Sequence

Instead of the V-I motion, this sequence imitates IV-I (the plagal cadence). The pattern: down a fourth, up a step, down a fourth, up a step.

![Pachelbel Sheet Music](/blog/music-theory-drills/pachelbel-sheet-music.jpg)

With one-octave arpeggios, this becomes the famous Canon in D progression. You'll find variations in Chopin's Tristesse and countless other pieces. Starting on the vi chord (relative minor) creates a more melancholic version.

## Arpeggio Progressions (14)

### Drill 14: Two-Octave Arpeggio Sequences

Take a simple progression (I-IV-V-I) and arpeggiate each chord over two octaves: up on I, down on IV, up on V, down to finish on I.

![Arpeggios Progression](/blog/music-theory-drills/arpeggios-progression.jpg)

The minor version (vi-ii-iii-vi) has a naturally melancholic quality. For classical flavor, try making the iii chord major (with a raised seventh)—this was common practice in the Classical period.

## Seventh Chords and Voicings (15-17)

Adding sevenths transforms triads into richer, more colorful harmonies.

### Drill 15: Diatonic Seventh Chords

Build seventh chords on each scale degree. The pattern: Maj7, min7, min7, Maj7, dom7, min7, half-diminished. Say each chord aloud as you play.

### Drill 16: Open Voicings

Spread your seventh chords across both hands for fuller, more pianistic sounds. Three versions:

**Open Voicing A**: Take a seventh chord and throw the third up an octave. Left hand plays root and fifth; right hand plays seventh and third.

**Open Voicing B**: Additionally throw the fifth up an octave, creating an even wider spread.

**Open Voicing C**: Play the root twice (octave apart) with the fifth in the left hand. Right hand plays two thirds (octave apart) filled with the seventh. This symmetrical voicing works beautifully for ballads and jazz standards.

### Drill 17: The Crab Walk

This is the cycle of fifths with seventh chords, using optimal voice leading. The magic: every chord shares two notes with the next.

Starting from Gmaj7 to Cmaj7: G and B are common tones, so keep them stationary and slide the other two voices down. Alternate which pair of voices moves, creating a "crab walk" motion down the keyboard.

The sequence: Gmaj7 - Cmaj7 - F#ø7 - Bm7 - Em7 - Am7 - D7 - Gmaj7. You land back on the tonic in a different inversion than you started.

## Advanced Harmony (18-20)

These final drills introduce chromatic alterations that add color and tension.

### Drill 18: Secondary Dominants

Every chord can have its own "mini-dominant" a fifth above it. The secondary dominant of ii (Am) is E7. The secondary dominant of iii (Bm) is F#7. And so on.

![Secondary Dominants](/blog/music-theory-drills/secondary-dominants.jpg)

Practice: I - V/ii - ii - V/iii - iii - V/IV - IV - V/V - V - V/vi - vi - V - I.

For smooth voice leading, put all secondary dominants in first inversion. This creates a chromatic ascending bass line: G - G# - A - A# - B - B - C - C# - D - D# - E - F# - G.

### Drill 19: Modal Interchange (Ascending Fourths)

What if we imitate IV-I instead of V-I, and keep going? Starting from I, go up a fourth to IV, then up another fourth... but this takes you outside the key.

The sequence: I - IV - bVII - bIII - bVI - bII - bV (and back down).

These "borrowed" chords come from parallel modes (G Mixolydian, G Dorian, etc.). With proper inversions, you get another smooth bass line. This is the technique Jacob Collier demonstrates frequently.

### Drill 20: Secondary ii-V-I Progressions

The ultimate challenge: approach each diatonic chord with its own ii-V-I.

Take the descending thirds progression (I-vi-IV-ii) and precede each target chord with its secondary ii and V:
- I
- ii/vi - V/vi - vi
- ii/IV - V/IV - IV
- ii/ii - V/ii - ii
- V - I

When the target is major, the secondary ii is minor seventh. When the target is minor, the secondary ii is half-diminished.

**Diabolical version**: Apply tritone substitution to every secondary V chord. The dominant's tritone substitute creates a chromatic descending bass line. This is advanced jazz harmony.

## Key Takeaways

- **Progress incrementally**: Master each drill before adding the next
- **Name chords aloud**: Build mental associations between sounds and labels
- **Apply voice leading**: Always look for common tones; move other voices minimally
- **Practice in multiple keys**: The real skill is fluency across all 12 keys
- **Think like a composer**: These drills are tools for making music, not ends in themselves

Start wherever you're comfortable. Return to this system periodically, adding one new drill to your practice. Over weeks and months, you'll develop the harmonic vocabulary that separates casual players from true musicians.
